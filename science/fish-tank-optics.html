Fish Tank Optics

// why the back wall sits closer than the ruler says

Put a ruler along the side of the tank and it reads 30 cm front to back. Then look through the front glass — and the back wall is plainly not 30 cm away. It looks about 22 cm away, and everything resting against it looks a size too big. Nothing has moved. Light took a bent path to reach you, and your eye refuses to believe it. This page takes that apart: why water slows light down, why slowing it makes it turn a corner, and why the one assumption your eye makes about light is right everywhere except here.

Part A · the thing you can actually see

01 · the missing 7 centimetres

A sticker on the inside of the back glass, seen straight on. It has not moved — but the light leaving the front glass is heading in a direction that points back to a spot 7.5 cm nearer.

01

Measure the tank with a ruler along the outside and you get the honest answer: 30 cm from the front glass to the back wall. A ruler does not care about light — it is touching both ends.

02

Look through the water instead and the back wall reports in at about 22.5 cm — three-quarters of the distance. Everything against it is about a size bigger than it should be at that range.

03

Tip the water out and the illusion goes with it. An empty tank measures right by eye. It is the water doing this, not the glass — and section 10 shows just how little the glass contributes.

04

The rule is simple and it applies to everything behind the front glass: a tank full of water steals a quarter of every distance measured through it. In a 30 cm tank that is 7.5 cm, gone.

The whole page in one line: light travels slower in water than in air, so it changes direction on the way out — and your eye, which has spent your whole life correctly assuming light comes in straight lines, traces it back along a straight line to the wrong place.

02 · you have already seen this three times

Same physics, three costumes. The straw, the coin and the pool floor all sit deeper than they look, by the same quarter.

01

A straw in a glass of water looks broken. The part under the water is not bent at all — it is just being shown to you raised up, closer to the surface than it really hangs.

02

A coin at the bottom of a cup floats up. Hide a coin behind the rim so you cannot see it, then pour water in without moving anything. The coin rises into view — light from it now leaves the surface on a new heading that clears the rim.

03

Every swimming pool looks shallower than it is. A 1.4 m pool reads about 1.05 m from the side. People who cannot swim step in expecting the shallow one, and find the deep one.

04

The fish tank is the same trick turned sideways. In a pool the water is between you and the floor; in a tank the water is between you and the back wall. Physics does not know which way is up — it only counts how much water the light had to cross.

Part B · what the light is doing

03 · light drives slower in water

The number written on each lane is its refractive index, n — and it is nothing more exciting than "how many times slower than in a vacuum". Water's 1.33 is where the missing quarter comes from: 1 ÷ 1.33 = 0.75.

01

In empty space light does 300,000 kilometres every second — the cosmic speed limit, and nothing beats it. Air is so thin it barely slows light at all, so air counts as 300,000 too.

02

Push light through water and it manages only about 225,000 km/s. Through the glass of the tank, about 197,000. It has not lost energy — the wave is simply being passed along by atom after atom, and each handover takes time.

03

We give that slowdown a name: the refractive index, n. It is just the speed in a vacuum divided by the speed in the stuff. n = 1.33 for water means light crawls along at 1 ÷ 1.33 = three-quarters speed.

04

The instant light leaves the water it is back to full speed. Nothing is damaged, nothing is used up — but a speed change at a boundary is exactly what makes a wave turn a corner, and that is the next section.

04 · slower on one side means it turns

Left, the everyday version: one end of the axle hits the fast surface first, outruns the other end, and the whole cart slews round. Right, the same thing happening to a light wave at the glass.

01

Roll a trolley off sand onto smooth tarmac at an angle. One wheel reaches the tarmac a moment before the other, speeds up while its twin is still dragging — and the trolley swings round to a new heading. Nobody steered it.

02

A light wave arrives at the glass as a long straight crest, not a dot. Hit the boundary square-on and every part of that crest crosses at the same instant — no swing, no bending at all. That is why a wall directly in front of you is displaced but not distorted.

03

Arrive at an angle, and one end of the crest gets out into the air first. It is now doing 300,000 km/s while the other end is still doing 225,000. The crest pivots, and the beam leaves on a new line.

04

Notice what does not change: the number of crests arriving per second. The colour of the light is fixed by that. So when the wave slows, the crests must bunch up — the wavelength shrinks by the same 1.33 — and when it speeds up again they spread back out.

Water to air bends light away from the straight-through line. Going the other way — air into water — it bends toward it. A useful way to remember which: light always swings so that it spends less time in the slow stuff. It is lazy, and it is very good at it.

Part C · what your eye does with it

06 · your eye only knows one rule, and it is wrong here

Two rays leave the same spot. Out in the air they are spreading faster than they were in the water — so traced backwards they cross early, short of the wall. Angles here are exaggerated roughly tenfold; the fan that really enters your pupil is about a degree wide, but every ray in it points back to the same near place.

01

A spot on the back wall throws light in every direction. Only a narrow fan of it happens to be aimed at your pupil, and that fan is what your eye has to work with.

02

The ray that leaves square-on does not bend at all. Its neighbours, arriving at the glass at a slight angle, get swung outwards — and the more off-square they were, the more they swing. The fan comes out wider than it went in.

03

Your eye now measures how fast that fan is spreading. That is genuinely all it has: a wide fan means near, a narrow fan means far. It is a good rule. You use it every second of every day.

04

So it runs the fan backwards in straight lines — because light has never once bent for you before — and finds where those lines cross. They cross short of the wall, and that is where the wall appears. Your eye is not malfunctioning. It is applying a correct rule to a situation the rule was never built for.

"So the fish is really somewhere else?" No — the fish is exactly where the ruler says. What is somewhere else is the image: a real, measurable place in space where the light behaves as though it started. Point a camera at it and the camera will focus there, not on the fish.

07 · the three-quarters rule

apparent distance = real distance ÷ 1.33 = real distance × 0.75 looking straight on · 1.33 is water's refractive index · works for any distance behind the front glass

The fraction never changes — only the amount. A tall tank loses as much depth as a long one, because it is the same water doing it.

01

The rule is divide by 1.33, which is the same as multiplying by 0.75. That is it. No lens, no focal length, no sweet spot — a flat pane of water treats every distance behind it identically.

02

It applies to the water path only. A poster on the wall behind the tank, seen through the tank, also jumps 7.5 cm closer — the water crossed is still 30 cm. But it is 7.5 cm out of two metres, so nobody notices.

03

The quarter you lose is fixed in centimetres, not in feel. Kneel with your nose on the glass and 7.5 cm is a third of everything you can see. Stand across the room and 7.5 cm is nothing. Same physics, wildly different impression.

04

Seawater is a touch denser in optical terms — n = 1.339 — so a reef tank steals about 25.3% instead of 25.0%. You will not spot the difference, but it is there.

08 · and it looks bigger, too

The image is not stretched — it is the same size the fish has always been. It is simply standing closer, and anything closer fills more of your view.

Test it in ten seconds. Pick a fish, put your nose on the glass, then walk slowly backwards without taking your eyes off it. It shrinks faster than it should — because you are losing the magnification at the same time as you are losing the angle. Divers know the pair by heart: through a flat mask, everything is a third bigger and a quarter closer.

09 · this is not "your brain being fooled"

01

Your two eyes agree. They converge — cross their aim — at 22.5 cm, not 30. That is a muscle movement, measurable with a ruler and a mirror. Nothing imaginary about it.

02

Parallax agrees. Slide your head sideways and watch the spot drift against the tank frame. It drifts by exactly the amount something 22.5 cm away would, not 30.

03

A camera agrees, and a camera has no imagination. Point one at the back wall and it racks its lens to 22.5 cm — and finds the wall perfectly sharp there. Underwater photographers plan around this — behind a flat port, the whole world is at three-quarters distance.

04

So the image is a genuine physical thing, not a misreading. The only thing that is wrong is the assumption "light came here in a straight line" — an assumption so reliable everywhere else that it is built into your eyes, your cameras and most of your intuition.

The honest summary: nothing lies to you. The water quietly changes where the light appears to start, and every instrument you own — eyes included — reports that new place correctly.
Part D · the glass, and what it does not do

10 · the glass is a slab, and a slab barely counts

Whatever the glass does on the way in, it undoes on the way out — because the two faces are parallel. The ray leaves on exactly the heading it arrived with.

01

The front pane has two parallel faces. Light bends toward the normal going in and by the identical amount away from it coming out. The turns cancel exactly. A parallel slab cannot change a ray's direction, no matter how thick it is or what it is made of.

02

What it can do is slide the ray sideways — and a sideways slide does move the image a little nearer. For typical 5 mm tank glass, that is about 1.7 mm. You will not see 1.7 mm.

03

There is a neater way to say it. For water → glass → air, the maths collapses: 1.33 × sin(water angle) = 1.52 × sin(glass angle) = 1.00 × sin(air angle). The glass term cancels out of both ends. The angle you finally see depends only on the water and the air.

04

Which is why acrylic tanks look exactly the same as glass ones (n = 1.49 against 1.52), and why a thick pane on a big aquarium does not distort more than a thin one on a nano tank.

11 · the layer-cake rule

apparent thickness = layer 1 ÷ n₁ + layer 2 ÷ n₂ + … a straight-on view stacks up, layer by layer — each one shrunk by its own n
5 mm glass ÷ 1.52 = 3.3 mm  ·  300 mm water ÷ 1.33 = 225 mm
305 mm really  →  228 mm as seen  =  76.7 mm nearer a 30 cm tank with 5 mm front glass · the glass contributes 1.7 of those 76.7 mm
This is why the empty-tank test is so convincing. Drain the tank and the back wall retreats by 7.5 cm — right back to where the ruler said it was, give or take the glass's 1.7 mm. The glass never moved. The water was doing all of it.

01

The three-quarters rule is a straight-on rule. Walk round to look along the tank instead of into it, and the far corner comes flying towards you — at a steep enough angle it sits at a third of its true distance.

02

Because the squeeze depends on the angle, and the angle is different for each part of the wall, a flat back wall stops looking flat. It bows gently towards you at the edges. Photographers shooting through a flat port fight this constantly — the corners of the frame are effectively closer than the middle.

03

Worse, at a steep angle there is no single sharp image left. The up-down squeeze and the left-right squeeze stop agreeing, so the image smears out. That is why a tank looks soft and greasy when you view it from the end, and crisp from the front.

04

Look through a corner, where two panes meet, and you can catch the same fish twice — once through the front glass and once through the side, at two different apparent distances. One fish, two ghosts, neither where the fish is.

The aquascaper's version of this section: a tank always shows less depth than it has, and the effect is worst where people actually stand. It is why layouts are built with strong foreground-to-background steps — the water has already flattened a quarter of the depth before anyone looks.

13 · past 48.6° the water turns into a mirror

Every angle gets stretched wider on the way out. At 48.6° the stretched angle reaches 90° — flat along the surface — and there is nowhere wider left to go.

01

Run the table from section 05 to its end: 30° becomes 41.8°, 40° becomes 59°, 45° becomes 70.5°. The escape angle is running out of room, and it hits the wall at 48.6°.

02

Beyond 48.6° — the critical angle for water and air — the sums ask for a sine bigger than 1, which does not exist. So none of the light escapes. All of it reflects, and it does so perfectly: no silver, no coating, better than any mirror you can buy.

03

You have seen this in your own tank. Look along the length of it and the front glass turns into a mirror with a second set of fish swimming in it. Look up at the underside of the water surface and there is another set, swimming upside down.

04

The glass makes no difference here either. Water → glass → air only escapes if 1.33 × sin(water angle) stays under 1 — the glass cancels again. 48.6° is a water-and-air number, and it is the same for a glass tank, an acrylic tank or a pond with no wall at all.

14 · and what does the fish see of you?

The same 48.6° that traps light inside the water also decides how much of the outside world gets in. Divers and fly fishers both know this circle well.

01

Flip the rule around and it flips its answer. For an observer in the water looking out, apparent distance = real distance × 1.33. You look further away to the fish than you really are, and correspondingly smaller.

02

So the courtesy is not mutual: you see the fish closer and bigger; the fish sees you further and smaller. Both of you are reading the light exactly right.

03

Looking up, the fish sees the whole sky squashed into a bright circle 97° wide — twice the critical angle. Everything from the horizon on one side to the horizon on the other is inside it, so the edges are badly crowded and the horizon is a smeared ring.

04

Outside that circle the surface is a perfect mirror showing the gravel and the fish's own tankmates. A fish in a lit room sees a bright porthole overhead and a silvered ceiling everywhere else.

15 · curved glass is a different animal

Schematic, not to scale — but the direction is right. Flat panes shift; curved panes focus.

01

Everything on this page so far assumed flat glass. A flat surface has no focal length — it cannot bring light to a point, so it can only slide images about by a fixed fraction.

02

Curve the glass and you have built a lens. A round goldfish bowl is a fat cylindrical lens full of water: it magnifies far more than 1.33×, it magnifies sideways more than up-and-down, and the fish visibly stretches and lurches as it swims across.

03

Bowfront and corner-curved tanks do a milder version of the same thing. The flat middle behaves; the curved ends bulge, and things appear to speed up as a fish crosses them.

04

One practical consequence: a round vase or bowl of water on a sunny windowsill is a burning glass. It really can focus enough sunlight to scorch a curtain or a windowsill. Flat-sided tanks cannot.

Part F · prove it yourself

16 · six things to try tonight

01 · half-fill it
Fill the tank only halfway and look at the back corner seam. Where it crosses the waterline it takes a visible step sideways — the submerged half is being shown to you nearer and wider.
02 · two stickers
Stick identical dots on the inside and the outside of the back glass, 5 mm apart. Through the water they look centimetres apart in depth, and the inner one looks bigger.
03 · nose on the glass
Press your face to the glass, pick one fish, then walk backwards slowly. It deflates as you go — you are giving up the magnification, which is biggest when your eye is closest.
04 · lean a ruler in
Stand a ruler diagonally in the tank. It looks snapped at the waterline. Nothing is bent — the underwater half is simply being displayed closer to the surface than it hangs.
05 · look along it
Put your eye near one end and sight down the length of the tank. The far glass silvers over and a full set of reflected fish appears — total internal reflection, free of charge.
06 · try to net a fish
You swipe short, every time. You are aiming at the image, and the fish is a quarter of the tank further back. Spear fishers have known this for thousands of years: aim beyond it.
The cheapest experiment of the lot: drop a coin in an empty mug, back off until the rim just hides it, hold still, and have someone pour water in. The coin climbs into view on its own. That is the fish tank's back wall doing exactly the same thing, lying on its side.
