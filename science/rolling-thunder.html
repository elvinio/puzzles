Rolling Thunder

// why a lightning strike doesn't just go BANG

01 · the channel & the listener

not to scale — illustrating relative distance & timing only

02 · what you actually hear, over time

01

The strike superheats the air. A lightning channel can hit roughly 30,000 K — hotter than the sun's surface — in a fraction of a second. That air expands explosively, sending out the shockwave we hear as thunder.

02

The channel is long, not a single point. A bolt plus its branches can stretch several kilometers across the sky. Every segment discharges at essentially the same instant and produces its own burst of sound.

03

Each segment is a different distance away. The nearest part — often the ground strike ① — is close, so its sound arrives almost immediately as a sharp crack. Segments higher up ④ or off to the side ③ are farther, so their sound arrives later — stretching one instantaneous event into several seconds of rumbling.

04

Distance also dulls the sound. High frequencies fade faster over distance than low ones. The near segment still sounds like a crisp crack, while sound from farther segments loses its sharp "snap" in transit and reaches you as a duller, lower rumble.

05

Echoes smear it even further. Reflections off clouds, terrain, and buildings add more delayed copies of the sound, blending everything into one continuous roll instead of distinct claps.

Why a nearby strike just goes CRACK: almost its whole channel is close to you, so almost all the sound arrives in one instant. A distant storm's channel is far away and spread out, so you're hearing it end-to-end — the far segments' sound simply takes noticeably longer to reach you than the near ones, smoothing it into a long, low rumble.
