Aquarium Life

// four kinds of living things sharing one small box of water

A planted tank is not an ornament. It is a tiny world with four crews living in it: guppies swimming about, cherry shrimp picking at the floor, snails sliding over the glass, and plants quietly turning light into food. Every one of them is born, eats, grows and makes more of itself — and each does it in a completely different way. This page shows you how.

Part A · meet the tank

01 · four neighbours, four ways of living

Four crews, four completely different body plans. Only one of them — the plant — can feed itself. The other three all have to go and find breakfast.

Here is the first surprising thing: these four are not just "tank animals". They belong to four different branches of life, about as far apart from each other as you are from a mushroom. A guppy is closer family to you than it is to a shrimp.

Fun fact: a woodlouse under a flowerpot and a cherry shrimp in your tank are cousins. Both are crustaceans. The woodlouse is basically a shrimp that crawled onto land and never went back.

02 · who lives where — the tank has floors

A tank is not one habitat, it is several stacked on top of each other. Each crew has a favourite floor, which is why they can all fit in 20 litres of water without getting in each other's way.

Guppies own the open water, snails own the surfaces, shrimp own the floor, and plants hold the whole thing together. Nobody is competing for the same spot.

The big idea: a good tank is a team. The fish make mess, the shrimp and snails tidy it into smaller mess, invisible bacteria turn that into plant food, and the plants turn it back into leaves and oxygen. You are the only one who has to remember to feed everybody.
Part B · guppies — the swimmers

03 · a guppy up close

A guppy is about 3–6 cm long — roughly your thumb. Everything on it has a job: fins to steer, gills to breathe, and colour to impress.

Guppies come from warm streams and ditches in South America. They are famously tough, famously busy, and famously good at making more guppies — which is why they are in almost every first tank in the world. Their other name is the millionfish, and you will find out why in section 06.

Fun fact: a fish "drinks" to breathe. Water carries dissolved oxygen. The guppy takes a mouthful, pushes it over the feathery gills inside the gill cover, and the gills grab the oxygen out of it — the same oxygen your lungs grab out of air, just delivered by water instead.

04 · boy or girl? — the easiest fish to tell apart

Look underneath, just past the belly. A thin pointed rod = male. A wide triangular fan = female. That one fin is the whole test.

In most animals you know, the male and female look fairly similar. In guppies they look like two different species. The male's pointed anal fin even has a name — the gonopodium — and it is not really a fin any more, it is a tube he uses to pass sperm to the female.

Why the boys are so bright: a female guppy chooses who to mate with, and she likes colour — especially orange. So over thousands of generations the flashiest males had the most babies, and their sons inherited the flash. The plain, careful females inherited something else: not getting eaten.

05 · the guppy life cycle — no egg stage at all

Four stages and no egg anywhere. A guppy goes from newborn to parent in about the time it takes you to get through one school term.

The whole trick in one line: guppies are livebearers. The eggs stay inside the mother, hatch inside her, and what comes out is a tiny fish that can already swim, steer and eat. Most fish — goldfish, tetras, angelfish — squirt eggs out and hope. Guppies skip that.

06 · guppy babies — born swimming

The whole point of being a livebearer: the babies skip the helpless egg stage and arrive ready to run. They still need somewhere to run to.

01
A male chases a female and, in about a second, uses his pointed fin to pass her a packet of sperm. Females do most of the choosing; males do most of the chasing.
02
The eggs are fertilised inside her and stay there. For roughly 4 weeks the babies grow in her belly, living off the yolk in their own eggs.
03
Her belly goes square and the gravid spot turns almost black. You are literally looking at dozens of tiny eyes through her skin. When the spot looks packed and her belly looks boxy, birth is a day or two away.
04
She gives birth to 20–50 fry (sometimes over 100 from a big old female), one at a time, usually in the early morning. Each one sinks for a second, then rights itself and swims off.
05
The fry hide instantly. They have to: adult guppies — including their own parents — will eat anything that fits in their mouth. In a bare tank almost none survive. In a mossy, planted tank, lots do.
06
About 4 weeks later she does it again — and she does not need to meet another male to do it.
Fun fact — the sperm bank: a female guppy can store sperm inside her body for months and use it batch after batch. One meeting with one male can produce five or six litters of babies. This is why a "single female" from a pet shop very often turns into thirty guppies at home.

Never tip unwanted fish or plants into a drain, a pond or a canal — escaped guppies have become a pest in warm countries all over the world.

07 · how guppies eat — the two-minute rule

More fish die from too much food than from too little. A guppy that misses a meal is fine. A guppy swimming in rotting leftovers is not.

Guppies are omnivores — they eat plants and animals. In a stream they nibble algae, pick tiny water-fleas out of the water and hoover up mosquito larvae. In your tank they eat whatever you drop in, and they will always act starving. They are lying.

Part C · cherry shrimp — the cleaners

08 · a cherry shrimp up close

A cherry shrimp is 2–3 cm — about as long as your thumbnail is wide. Everything about it says "small, busy, and made of armour."

Cherry shrimp have no bones inside at all. Their skeleton is the shell on the outside, like a suit of armour with hinges at the joints. That works brilliantly — until they need to get bigger. We will come back to that in section 11, because it is the strangest thing in the tank.

Fun fact: wild cherry shrimp are muddy brown. Every bright red one in a shop is descended from a few oddly-coloured shrimp that people bred together on purpose, over and over. Blue, yellow, orange and black ones came from the same trick. It is the same thing humans did to wolves to get spaniels.

09 · the shrimp life cycle — a baby is just a tiny adult

Notice what is missing: there is no tadpole stage, no caterpillar stage, no drifting larva. A cherry shrimp hatches as a shrimp and just gets bigger.

Why that matters: most shrimp in the sea hatch as drifting larvae that look nothing like their parents and need salty water to survive. Cherry shrimp skipped that step, so their whole life fits inside your freshwater tank. That single difference is why they breed happily at home and their cousins the Amano shrimp almost never do — Amano babies hatch as larvae and die unless they reach the sea.

10 · shrimp babies — mum carries them the whole time

A guppy mother carries her babies inside for a month. A shrimp mother carries hers outside, stuck to her own legs, for about the same month — and cleans every single egg by hand.

01
The saddle. A yellow or greenish patch shows up on the female's back, just behind her head. It looks like a tiny saddle. Those are her eggs, ripening inside her body.
02
She moults. To mate she first has to shed her shell. As the old shell comes off she releases a chemical into the water — a smell that means "I'm ready."
03
The shrimp dance. Every male picks up the scent and starts swimming laps of the tank at top speed, hunting for her. If your calm little shrimp suddenly go bananas for ten minutes, this is why.
04
Berried. The fertilised eggs move down under her tail and she glues them to her swimmerets. A female carrying eggs is called berried, because the clump looks like a bunch of tiny berries.
05
Thirty days of fanning. She waves her swimmerets constantly to wash fresh water over the eggs. Stop fanning and they would suffocate or go mouldy. Watch closely and you can see the eggs change from yellow to dark — that is the babies' eyes forming, just like in a guppy.
06
Hatching. 20–30 shrimplets pop out over a few hours, each about 2 mm. They are already hungry and already grazing. Mum's job is over.
Spot the babies: newly hatched shrimplets are nearly see-through and only 2 mm long, so you will not notice them for weeks. Then one day you look at the moss and realise it is moving. That is the moment every shrimp keeper remembers.

11 · changing armour — the weirdest thing in your tank

Your skeleton is inside you and grows with you. A shrimp's skeleton is a hard shell on the outside, and hard shells cannot stretch. So to get bigger, a shrimp has to climb out of its own body armour and grow a new, roomier one. This is called moulting, and a young shrimp does it every week or two.

Find a see-through shrimp lying on the gravel and do not panic. Pick it up: if it is hollow and weightless, it is an old coat, not a body.

Fun fact — how to tell a moult from a dead shrimp: a moult is clear or white and see-through, and there is usually a clean gap between the head shield and the tail where it split. A dead shrimp keeps its colour and often turns pinkish-orange, like a cooked prawn. Leave the moult in the tank — the other shrimp will eat it for the calcium.
Shrimp need minerals. Building a brand-new shell every couple of weeks takes calcium, which they get from the water and from food. In very soft water shrimp get stuck half-out of their old shell — keepers call it "the white ring of death". A bit of mineral hardness in the water fixes it.

12 · how shrimp eat — the endless picnic

Shrimp are detritivores — "rubbish eaters". They spend their whole life picking through the leftovers of everything else in the tank.

01
A shrimp's two front arms end in little fan-shaped brushes. It touches a surface, the bristles rake up whatever is there, and the arms fold it into the mouth. Then it does it again. Roughly forever.
02
Most of what they eat is biofilm — the invisible slimy carpet of bacteria and tiny algae that grows on every leaf, rock and pane of glass. A brand-new, spotlessly clean tank is actually a starving tank for shrimp.
03
They also eat fallen flakes, dead leaves, dead shrimp, their own old shells and, brilliantly, the fish poo they can break down further. Nothing in a tank is wasted.
04
Extras that make them very happy: blanched courgette, spinach or carrot; a dried indian almond leaf; a shrimp pellet once or twice a week. One pellet. A whole spoonful will rot before they finish it.
The one thing that kills shrimp: copper. It is harmless to fish at the doses used in many fish medicines and snail-killers, but it poisons every shrimp in the tank. Read the label on anything you pour in. If it says copper, it is not going anywhere near a shrimp tank.
Part D · snails — the gliders

13 · a snail up close

A snail is basically a stomach riding on a muscle, wearing a house. The house is not a shelter it found — it is part of the animal, grown from its own body.

That single foot is not sliding, it is walking. Ripples of muscle run along the underside from back to front, like a tiny wave, and the snail rides them forward on a carpet of its own slime. Watch one from underneath through the glass and you can see the ripples moving.

Fun fact: snail slime is so good at gripping that a snail can crawl upside down along the underside of the water surface. Not on the glass — on the water itself, hanging from the surface skin. Look for it after the lights go on.

14 · a tongue with thousands of teeth

A snail does not bite. It files. The radula is a ribbon covered in rows of microscopic teeth that it drags across a surface like sandpaper, over and over.

Fun fact: a small aquarium snail carries thousands of teeth — many rows of them, all on one ribbon. They wear out in days, so the ribbon is constantly making new ones at the back and shedding the ruined ones at the front. It is a conveyor belt of teeth, and it never stops.
Listen for it. Put your ear close to a quiet tank at night, or watch a snail on the glass from the other side. You can actually see the little dark radula flicking in and out of the mouth, scrubbing the same spot again and again before it moves on.

15 · snail eggs — three snails, three completely different plans

Same job, three totally different answers. The eggs a snail lays tell you more about it than its shell does.

01
Eggs go on a surface, not inside a parent. Every one of these snails glues its eggs onto something — a rock, a leaf, the glass — and then walks away. No fanning, no guarding, no carrying. That is the end of parenting.
02
Two to four weeks later, tiny snails hatch out. And they really are tiny snails — complete with a perfect, see-through shell the size of a full stop. Like shrimp, there is no in-between shape.
03
The shell grows with them. A snail never swaps shells. It adds new shell material around the opening, a little every day, so the shell spirals outwards and gets bigger. The oldest, smallest part is right in the middle of the swirl — that tip was its baby shell.
04
Most tank snails are grown up in 2–3 months and live for about a year (nerites and mystery snails often longer).
Fun fact — the swirl is a diary: look at a snail's shell in good light and you can see faint growth lines, like tree rings. Rough, thin or pitted patches are the weeks when there was not enough calcium in the water. You are reading the snail's history of dinners.

16 · how snails eat — and what a snail boom is telling you

Snails cannot make babies out of nothing. Every extra snail was built out of extra food. If they are everywhere, they are eating something — and you are the one putting it there.

So don't poison them — feed less. Snail-killing chemicals also kill your shrimp, and the dead snails rot and poison the water. Cut the food for a week and the population sorts itself out. A tank with a steady, small number of snails is a tank that is being fed properly.
Fun fact — the cucumber trick: if you really do have too many, put a slice of blanched courgette in at night. In the morning it will be covered in snails, and you can lift the whole lot out in one go. Easier than a chemical, and the shrimp get a snack out of it too.
Part E · plants — the living furniture

17 · plants are alive too — and they cook their own dinner

Plants do not eat food. They build it, out of light, water and a gas. Then they burn it again at night, the same way your body burns breakfast.

This is why light matters so much in a tank. Too little light and the plants starve and melt away. Too much light and the algae — which are also plants, just very small ones — grow faster than anything else and cover everything. Around 6–8 hours a day on a timer is the sweet spot, and a timer beats remembering.

Fun fact — pearling: on a bright afternoon a healthy plant makes oxygen faster than the water can dissolve it, so the extra collects into tiny silver bubbles that cling to the leaves and then let go and float up. Aquarium people call this pearling. It is the plant literally showing you it is having a good day.

18 · how plants "eat" — roots, leaves, and one rule that matters

Burying an anubias or a java fern up to its thick stem is the single most common way beginners kill a plant that is otherwise almost unkillable.

Here is the loop that makes the whole tank work. Fish make ammonia. Invisible bacteria turn ammonia into nitrite and then into nitrate. Nitrate is a fertiliser — and plants drink it. So the plants are quietly eating the fish's waste and handing back oxygen and hiding places. A well-planted tank stays cleaner for longer, all by itself.

Plants also eat the dangerous stuff first. Given the choice, many water plants will grab ammonia straight out of the water — the most poisonous form of fish waste — before they bother with nitrate. A thicket of fast-growing plants is a safety net for your fish.

19 · plant babies — no eggs, no seeds, just copies

Four ways to make a new plant, and not a seed among them. Every one of these babies is a perfect copy of the parent — a clone.

Animals in your tank need two parents (or at least one parent with stored sperm). Most aquarium plants do not bother. They just grow an extra piece of themselves and let it go. Scientists call this asexual reproduction — "making babies without mixing".

20 · five plants that are very hard to kill

JAVA FERN
tie to wood · low light
Grows babies on its own leaf tips. Snails and fish ignore it — the leaves taste horrible. Never bury the thick stem.
ANUBIAS
tie to rock · very low light
Thick leathery leaves that grow slowly and last for years. Cut the stem in half to make two. Also never bury it.
HORNWORT
float it · grows like mad
No roots at all — it just floats and grows. Eats nitrate fast, shades the tank, and makes a brilliant jungle for baby guppies.
JAVA MOSS
drop it anywhere
The shrimp nursery. Baby shrimp and guppy fry live inside it, and it is full of the biofilm they eat. Grows on anything.
AMAZON SWORD
plant in gravel · feed the roots
The big centrepiece plant. Sends out runners with new plants along them. Needs a root tab pushed into the gravel now and then.
Never put aquarium plants in a stream, pond or drain. Hornwort, elodea and duckweed are brilliant in a tank and disasters in the wild — they choke real waterways and are extremely hard to remove. Spare plants go in the bin or to another fishkeeper, not into nature.
Part F · the whole team

The loop is not perfect. Nitrate still builds up slowly, and only one thing takes it out for good: you, changing a bucket of water every week. In a river the water is always leaving. In a tank, you are the river. (There is a whole page about that — see Fish Tank in the Science section.)

22 · fun things to actually try

All of these need nothing but your eyes, a bit of patience and sometimes a stopwatch. A notebook makes them ten times better, because then you are not just looking — you are doing science.

01The bubble count
Pick one plant leaf. Count the oxygen bubbles that leave it in one minute, on a bright day and on a dull day. Then do it an hour after the lights go off. Graph all three.
02Snail speed record
Stick two bits of tape on the glass, 10 cm apart. Time a snail crossing between them. Work out its speed in cm per minute — then how long it would take to cross your bedroom.
03Watch the teeth
Find a snail on the glass and look at it from the other side. You are looking straight at its mouth. Watch the dark little radula flick out, scrape, and pull back. Over and over.
04Ghost hunt
Look for a see-through shrimp lying on the gravel. Is it hollow and split behind the head? Then it is a moult, not a body. Keep a tally — a moult a week means your shrimp are growing.
05Predict the birthday
Photograph a pregnant guppy's gravid spot at the same time each day. When it looks black and packed and her belly goes square, write down your prediction. See how close you get.
06The courgette race
Drop in a slice of blanched courgette and time who arrives first: shrimp, snail or guppy? Repeat on three different days. Does the same crew always win?
07The two-minute test
Feed your normal pinch and start a timer. Is it all gone in 2 minutes? If not, you are feeding too much. Halve it tomorrow and test again.
08Plant growth chart
Put a sticker on the glass at the tip of your tallest plant. Add a new sticker every Sunday. After two months you have a growth chart on the side of the tank.
09Make two from one
Snap a hornwort stem in half, or cut an anubias rhizome in two. Plant both pieces. In a month you have two plants that are genetically identical — you have made a clone.
10Boy or girl census
Count your guppies and sort them by anal fin shape: pointed rod or wide fan. Write down the ratio. Check again in two months and see what changed.
11The moss safari
Stare into one clump of java moss for three whole minutes without looking away. Baby shrimp and guppy fry are almost invisible until your eyes adjust. Then suddenly the moss is full of them.
12Lights-on stampede
Watch the tank for the first 60 seconds after the light comes on. Who moves first, who hides, who heads for the surface? Write the order down. It is nearly the same every day.
Keep a tank diary. One line a day — what you fed, what you saw, anything odd. Two things happen. You start noticing patterns nobody told you about, and when something goes wrong you have the evidence to work out why. That is exactly what real scientists do.

23 · wow facts to win an argument with

A guppy is born ready
No egg, no helpless stage. A newborn guppy can swim, steer and hunt for food within seconds of being born.
One mating, six families
A female guppy stores sperm for months and can produce litter after litter without ever meeting another male.
Guppies fight malaria
They were shipped all over the world on purpose, because they eat mosquito larvae. That is also why they are now an invasive pest in many warm countries.
A shrimp climbs out of itself
It splits its shell behind the head and walks out backwards — leaving a perfect clear copy of itself, eye coverings and all, lying on the gravel.
Shrimp eat their own clothes
The discarded shell is full of calcium, so they come back and eat it to build the next one.
The shrimp disco
When a female moults she releases a scent, and every male in the tank starts racing in circles hunting for her. It lasts about half an hour.
Nerite eggs never hatch
They stick their white eggs on everything, but the babies need salty water. In your freshwater tank not a single one will ever hatch — so nerites can never take over.
Some snails are both
A bladder snail is male and female at the same time. One single snail, on its own, can start an entire population.
Thousands of teeth
A snail's radula carries thousands of microscopic teeth on a ribbon that is constantly replaced — new ones at the back, worn-out ones crumbling off the front.
Snails walk on water
They can glide upside down along the underside of the water surface, hanging from the surface skin.
A plant shows off
On a really bright day a healthy plant makes oxygen faster than the water can absorb it, and silver bubbles stream off the leaves. That is called pearling.
Plants breathe at night
In the dark, plants use oxygen and give out carbon dioxide — exactly like you. Which is why a tank stuffed with plants and no water movement is riskiest just before dawn.
Ferns with babies on their leaves
A java fern grows complete tiny ferns along the edge of its own leaves. They drop off, float away and land somewhere new.
Duckweed doubles
In good light it can double its numbers in two days. Ten leaves can become thousands within a month.
Your woodlouse is a shrimp
Woodlice are crustaceans — land relatives of the cherry shrimp. They still breathe with gill-like plates, which is why they need damp places.

The one-sentence version: guppies give birth to swimmers, shrimp carry their eggs and climb out of their own armour, snails glue their eggs to the glass and file their dinner with a belt of teeth, and plants just quietly grow copies of themselves out of light — and all four of them are living in the same 20 litres of water, getting along.

Numbers here are typical for a small warm freshwater tank and are rounded for learning. Real animals vary — that is half the fun of watching them. Always check the instructions on your own food, conditioner and test kit, and ask an adult before changing anything about the tank.
