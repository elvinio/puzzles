Liquid Motion Bubbler

// two liquids that refuse to mix, falling through each other in slow motion

Turn one over and it starts: fat coloured drops peel off a little shelf, sink through the clear liquid as if they were falling through honey, and puddle at the bottom — while blobs of the other liquid climb quietly the other way. Nothing is powered. Nothing is pumping. It is just two liquids swapping places, very slowly, and never blending. Three ideas explain everything you can see: polarity (why they never mix), density (who ends up on top) and surface tension (why the falling liquid travels as round drops instead of a smear).

Part A · what you're looking at

01 · inside the tube

A real bubbler may have several shelves and cups instead of one — but this is the whole machine: two liquids, gravity, and a small hole.

01

There are exactly two liquids in there, and nothing else. One is oily and light. One is watery and heavy. There is no motor, no battery, no pump — the only thing that ever happens to a bubbler is that somebody turns it upside down.

02

They will not mix, no matter how long you wait or how hard you shake it. Shake it and you get a cloudy mess of tiny droplets — then, minute by minute, the droplets find each other and the two clean layers come back. That's polarity, and it's Part B.

03

One of them is heavier for its size than the other, so it always ends up underneath. That's density, and it's Part C.

04

The heavy liquid travels as round drops with a crisp edge instead of a spreading smear. That's surface tension, and it's Part D.

The whole toy in one line: you lift the heavy liquid to the top with your arm, and gravity spends the next few minutes putting it back down — drop by drop, through a liquid it cannot dissolve into.

02 · the flip — an upside-down stack

Flipping it doesn't start a machine. It stacks the liquids the wrong way round, and everything after that is gravity undoing your work.

01

Heavy-on-the-bottom is the restful arrangement. Left alone, any two liquids that won't mix will settle heavy-under-light and stay there forever. Nothing is pushing them; it is simply the arrangement with the least energy in it.

02

Turning it over is you doing work. Your arm lifts the heavy liquid to the top of the tube. That takes real energy, and the tube now stores it, exactly like a wound-up spring or a lifted weight.

03

A heavy layer sitting on a light one is unstable. In an empty pipe it would collapse in a chaotic, sloshing rush. The cups, holes and shelves inside a bubbler are there to slow that collapse down and turn it into a tidy queue of drops.

04

Nothing is lost, so it stops. When the last drop has landed, the stack is restful again and the tube goes still — until somebody lifts it back up. The energy you put in came out as a tiny amount of heat in the liquid, warming it by roughly a thousandth of a degree.

Part B · polarity

03 · water is a lopsided molecule

Polar means "has a positive end and a negative end". Water is the classic example — and that one fact runs the whole toy.

01

Atoms share electrons unfairly. In a water molecule the oxygen atom pulls the shared electrons much harder than the two hydrogens do. The electrons spend more time near the oxygen, so that end is left slightly negative and the hydrogen ends are left slightly positive. "Slightly" is what the little δ means.

02

The shape matters too. Water is bent, like a boomerang, not straight. If it were straight the two pulls would point opposite ways and cancel out. Bent, they add up — so the whole molecule behaves like a minuscule magnet with a plus end and a minus end.

03

So water molecules cling to each other. The plus end of one reaches for the minus end of the next, over and over, in every direction. Water isn't a crowd of loose molecules — it's a wobbling net, re-linking itself trillions of times a second.

04

Each grip is weak — about one twentieth the strength of the bonds holding a molecule together — but there are an unimaginable number of them. That's why water is a liquid at all at room temperature, why it beads on a car bonnet, and why it will shortly refuse to let any oil in.

04 · oil is smooth and even

Non-polar means "no ends". An oil molecule is carbon and hydrogen all the way along, sharing electrons fairly, with nothing sticking out for water to grab.

01

Carbon and hydrogen pull almost equally. Neither one wins the tug-of-war for the electrons, so there's no lopsided end anywhere on the molecule. Oil isn't a magnet; it's a plain grey stick.

02

Oil molecules do still stick together — faintly. Electrons jiggle about, so for a split second one part of a molecule is a hair more negative than another, and its neighbour feels it. These flickering grips are real, but they're far weaker than water's.

03

That weakness is why oil feels slippery and runny compared to a sticky syrup, and it's also why it will lose the coming argument with water — and end up pushed out of the way.

05 · like sticks to like — so oil gets squeezed out

Shake a bubbler and you run this film from left to right — it always ends the same way.

01

To mix, water would have to let go of water. Slipping an oil molecule into the water means prising apart a patch of that hydrogen-bonded net — and the oil gives nothing back, because it has no ends to grip with. The swap is a bad deal, so it doesn't happen.

02

So the water shuts the oil out and the oil, having nowhere else to go, gathers with its own kind. Chemists put it in four words: like dissolves like. Polar dissolves polar, non-polar dissolves non-polar, and across that line, almost nothing.

03

Small droplets merge into bigger ones because every merge shrinks the amount of unwanted oil-and-water contact. The end point is one single flat boundary — the least contact the two can possibly have. That flat line in your bubbler is the finish line.

04

Soap breaks the rule — and shows how strong it is. A soap molecule is a cheat: a water-loving head on an oil-loving tail. It wraps each oil droplet in a coat of water-friendly heads, and suddenly the grease on a plate can leave with the water. Your bubbler contains no soap, which is exactly why it still works years later.

The myth: "oil and water repel each other." They don't. Two oil molecules and a water molecule attract each other perfectly politely — just feebly. Water simply prefers its own company so much more that the oil gets crowded out. Being left out is not the same as being pushed away.

06 · the colour proves it

You can check polarity with your eyes: a dye dissolves in the layer it matches, and only that one.

Why your bubbler is coloured at all: a drop of clear liquid falling through another clear liquid is nearly invisible — the two bend light almost the same way. Dye one of them and every drop, every rising blob and the boundary itself suddenly has an edge you can follow. The colour isn't decoration; it's what makes the physics visible.
Part C · density

07 · density is "how much stuff is packed in"

Density = weight ÷ size. Numbers here are grams per cubic centimetre, rounded; real oils run from about 0.8 to 0.96.

01

Heavy and light are the wrong words — a bathtub of oil outweighs a spoonful of water. What matters is weight for a given size. That's density, and comparing densities tells you exactly which liquid ends up on top.

02

Oil is less dense than water for two reasons: its molecules are mostly light carbon and hydrogen, and their long tangled chains can't pack as neatly as water's tight little net of hydrogen bonds. Less stuff per box.

03

Stacking is automatic. Because the two liquids won't blend (Part B), gravity is free to sort them, and it always reaches the same answer: densest at the bottom. Pour them in backwards and they'll simply trade places while you watch.

08 · the tug-of-war on every drop

Arrows drawn to scale for a drop of density 1.00 sinking through an oil of 0.85. Flip the two densities over and the leftover arrow points upward instead — that's a rising bubble.

what's left to move the drop  =  ( heavy density − light density ) × size of drop × gravity this is Archimedes' principle, written as a subtraction
01

Gravity pulls on the drop — no surprise. That's the weight arrow, and it depends on how dense the drop's liquid is.

02

But the drop is not falling through nothing. To move down it has to shove oil out of the way, and the oil pushes back, upward, with exactly the weight of the oil that got displaced. That's upthrust — the same reason a beach ball is hard to hold under water.

03

The two nearly cancel. What's left — the difference in the two densities — is all that actually drives the drop downward. Not its weight. The difference.

04

And that's the design secret of the whole toy. Choose two liquids whose densities are close, and the leftover push becomes tiny. The drop still falls — it just takes its time about it.

09 · the tiny gap is the whole trick

Same drop, same gravity, three different surroundings. Only the size of the density gap has changed.

Why slow motion is a design choice, not a coincidence: a maker who picks two liquids with densities of 1.00 and 0.85 has thrown away 85% of the drop's weight before it even starts. Push the two numbers closer still and the drops crawl; push them apart and the toy is over in seconds. Manufacturers tune that gap on purpose.
Try it: hold the tube in your warm hands for a minute and it usually speeds up. Heat makes the oil runnier, and it also swells the two liquids by different amounts, which nudges the density gap. Put it somewhere cold and it slows to a crawl. Same toy, same gravity — different numbers.
Part D · surface tension

10 · why a drop is a ball

01

A molecule in the middle is happy; a molecule on the edge is not. In the middle it's gripped from every direction. At the boundary with the oil, half its neighbours are strangers it can't grip properly. Edge molecules are, in a sense, short-changed.

02

So the boundary behaves like a stretched sheet of elastic. That pull is surface tension. It isn't a skin made of anything — there's no extra material there — it's just the natural tightening of a surface that would rather be smaller.

03

Fewer strangers means a smaller boundary, and the shape with the least boundary for a given amount of liquid is a sphere. So a free-floating drop is a ball. Gravity can squash a big drop a little, but in a bubbler even that is gentle — the drops stay startlingly round.

04

It's the boundary between the two liquids that counts here — not water meeting air. Chemists call it interfacial tension. It's usually weaker than a water-and-air surface, because oil at least half-heartedly grips the water. Weaker pull, gentler drops, softer wobbles.

11 · how big a drop gets before it lets go

The rim can only hold so much. Everything about a drop's size follows from where those two lines cross.

drop weight when it falls  ≈  surface tension × distance around the hole bigger hole → bigger drops · stronger surface tension → bigger drops · bigger density gap → smaller drops
01

A drop hanging from a hole is held up by its own rim. Surface tension pulls upward all the way around the circle where the liquid meets the lip. That grip is a fixed size — it depends on the tension and on how far it is around the hole, and on nothing else.

02

Meanwhile the drop is filling up and its weight climbs steadily. Sooner or later weight passes the grip — and that moment is not a matter of opinion. It happens at the same size every single time.

03

Then the neck pinches shut. The thin waist between the drop and the hole is the most expensive shape in the whole arrangement, so the boundary snaps it closed and the drop leaves — often with a tiny satellite droplet trailing behind it, a straggler pinched off from the neck itself.

04

That's why every drop in a bubbler looks the same size. It isn't careful manufacturing — it's the same sum being solved over and over. Scientists once measured surface tension exactly this way: count the drops from a known nozzle, weigh them, work backwards.

12 · why a stream turns into beads

Once a wobble starts it only ever gets bigger — and the beads end up roughly twice as wide as the thread they came from.

01

A long thin thread is expensive. Stretch liquid into a tube and you've made an enormous amount of boundary for a very small amount of liquid. The same liquid balled up into beads has much less. The thread is sitting on a hill, waiting to roll off it.

02

Any tiny wobble grows. A spot that happens to be a hair narrower gets squeezed harder, so liquid drains out of it into the fatter parts either side — which makes it narrower still. There is no way back. The wobble runs away with itself until the thin spots close completely.

03

This is the Plateau–Rayleigh instability, and it is everywhere once you notice it: a tap left barely running breaks into drops, a spider's silk turns into a necklace of dew beads, and a bubbler refuses to let the heavy liquid run down as a stream. It always chooses drops.

13 · the wall is oily, so nothing sticks

01

Wetting is a three-way contest. At the wall, the oil and the heavy liquid are competing for the plastic. Whichever grips it better spreads out and coats it; the loser has to bead up and keep its distance.

02

In a bubbler the oil wins, because the plastic the tube is made of is mostly carbon-and-hydrogen chains too — much closer to the oil’s family than to water’s. Like sticks to like all over again, this time between a liquid and a solid.

03

So a permanent film of oil lines the wall and the drops ride down on it without ever touching plastic. That's why the tube stays clear, why drops keep their shape all the way down, and why a bubbler looks as sharp on its thousandth flip as on its first.

The same idea, in a frying pan: water beads and skitters on a well-oiled pan because the oil got to the metal first. Wetting is just polarity, applied to a surface.
Part E · why it's so slow

14 · three dials set the speed

sinking speed  ∝  ( drop width × drop width × density gap ) ÷ thickness of the oil Stokes' law — it holds whenever things move slowly and smoothly, which is exactly what a bubbler does
01

A falling drop doesn't keep speeding up. The faster it goes, the harder the oil drags on it — and within a blink the drag exactly balances the leftover push from Part C. From then on the drop travels at a steady crawl the whole way down. That steadiness is a big part of why it's so soothing to watch: no acceleration, no drama.

02

Size is the strongest dial — its effect is squared. A drop twice as wide sinks four times as fast. Watch a bubbler closely and you'll see the small stragglers left far behind by the fat drops, all falling through the very same liquid.

03

Thickness — viscosity — is the brake, and it's the one most sensitive to temperature. Warm oil can be several times runnier than cold oil, which is why the same bubbler runs briskly in a sunny window and sluggishly on a cold shelf.

04

The maker turns all three dials at once: a small hole to make small drops, a thickish oil, and two densities chosen to sit close together. Three separate brakes, multiplied — and a drop that takes a whole leisurely breath to cross the tube.

15 · no splashing, no swirls, no mixing

The Reynolds number compares how much a flow wants to keep barrelling along against how much the liquid's thickness damps it out. Small number, no swirls.

01

Fast things in thin fluids make a mess. A raindrop rips a churning wake of eddies behind it. It wobbles, it tumbles, and the air behind it is disturbed long after it's gone.

02

Slow things in thick fluids glide. In a bubbler the oil's thickness damps out every swirl before it can form. The oil parts in front of the drop and closes up behind it as if nothing had happened. Physicists call this creeping flow — it is nearly the gentlest kind of motion there is.

03

And because there are no swirls, there is no stirring. Without turbulence to churn them together, and with the two liquids refusing to dissolve into each other anyway, the drop's edge stays razor sharp for its entire journey. That crisp blue line is polarity and smooth flow, working together.

04

Bonus: a liquid drop is not a solid bead. The oil dragging past its outside sets the liquid inside rolling, like the tread of a tank turning over. That internal roll lets the drop slip through a little faster than a solid ball of the same size and weight would manage.

16 · the rhythm — regular, but never quite

Each tick is one drop. The gaps stretch as the run goes on — the bubbler winds down instead of stopping dead.

01

The cup is the metronome. Liquid can only leave through the hole, and the hole only lets go one drop at a time, at the size fixed back in section 11. That turns a would-be rush into a beat.

02

The beat slows as the cup drains. A deep pool of liquid presses harder on the hole than a shallow one, so drops form quickly at first. As the level falls the push weakens, the drops take longer to build, and the gaps stretch out. The last few take ages.

03

And no two runs are identical. A drop that catches another on the way down arrives as one fat drop travelling faster. A stray bubble nudges a drop off course. The order is set by physics, but the details are set by chance — which is precisely why watching it never gets dull.

Why your eyes like it so much: the rhythm is almost predictable. Regular enough that your brain relaxes and stops bracing for a surprise, irregular enough that it never quite finishes the job of predicting and wanders off. Fires, rain on a window and waves on a beach all do the same thing.
Part F · watch it like a scientist

17 · six things to spot on the next flip

the hesitating drop
It lands on the pool and just sits there — then suddenly vanishes into it. A whisker-thin film of oil is trapped underneath, and it has to squeeze out before the two can touch.
the cascade
Sometimes a drop only half-merges, leaving a smaller copy of itself bobbing on the surface — which half-merges again, and again, a shrinking staircase of drops.
the chase
A drop following in another's slipstream catches it up and merges. The new fat drop is wider — so it sinks faster than either of its parents, and pulls away.
the satellite
When the neck snaps, it often pinches twice and leaves a miniature drop behind. Small means slow — it trails the big one all the way down and arrives long after.
the wobble
A just-released drop pulses between squashed and stretched for a second or two. Surface tension overshoots the round shape, like a struck bell ringing, before it settles.
the climbers
The blobs going up are the oil — the same physics with the signs reversed. They're what put the "bubbler" in the name, and almost nobody watches them.
Two experiments you can run in ten seconds. Cup it in your hands for a minute, then set it down and flip: it should run noticeably quicker while it's warm. And tilt it slightly instead of standing it upright — the drops have a longer, shallower slope to travel and a wall to hug, and the whole run stretches out.

The three big ideas
In one line
Polarity
Water has a plus end and a minus end and grips itself; oil has neither, so it gets crowded out. This is why there are two liquids at all.
Density
Only the difference between the two densities pushes a drop — make it small and the drop crawls. This is why it's slow.
Surface tension
A boundary costs energy, so the liquid keeps it as small as it can. This is why it's drops, and why they're round.
The one-sentence version: two liquids that can't stand to mix are stacked the wrong way up, and gravity spends the next few minutes putting them right — one drop at a time, each drop rounded by its own boundary, each one falling with only the difference between two densities to pull it down.

Numbers on this page are typical values, rounded for learning — the exact liquids inside a bubbler vary from maker to maker, and are usually kept secret. Bubblers are sealed for a reason: the liquids inside are not meant to be drunk, and a cracked one should be thrown away rather than opened.
