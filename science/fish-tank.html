Fish Tank

// how a small tank stays alive — a beginner's guide to a 20 litre tank

A fish tank looks simple: glass, water, fish. But the water is doing a job. Fish make waste, waste turns into poison, and tiny invisible helpers turn that poison into something safe. Your job is to keep that chain working. This page explains how, in plain words.

Part A · the big picture

01 · your tank is a closed loop

A river is an open loop — water flows through it. Your tank is a closed loop — the same water goes round and round for ever.

01

In a river, water only passes through. Fresh water arrives at one end and leaves at the other, carrying the fish waste away with it. The fish never swim in yesterday's mess. That is an open loop: stuff comes in, stuff goes out, nothing builds up.

02

In a tank, the water goes in circles. The filter pulls water in and pushes the same water back out. Nothing new arrives, nothing leaves. That is a closed loop. Every flake of food, every poop, every drop of medicine stays in the box.

03

So you are the river. The only way waste leaves a tank is if you take it out — by changing water, cleaning the filter, trimming plants, or scooping out leftovers. A filter does not remove waste from the tank; it just holds it in one place and changes it into something safer.

04

One part really is open: the water surface. Oxygen comes in there and carbon dioxide goes out there. That is why a rippling surface (from the filter outlet or an air stone) keeps fish healthier than still, glassy water.

05

Small loop = fast trouble. A 20 litre tank is a very small closed loop. The same mistake that a 200 litre tank shrugs off — one extra feed, one dead snail, a hot afternoon — can change a 20 litre tank in hours. Small tanks need more care, not less.

Remember this one idea: everything you add stays in the water until you remove it or a living thing eats it. The rest of this page is just the two ways that happens — bacteria change it, and you take it out.

02 · the nitrogen cycle — the whole story on one page

Read it top to bottom. Poison (red) → still poison (amber) → almost harmless (green) → out of the tank. The two purple steps are done by bacteria, free of charge.

A

Waste becomes ammonia. Fish breathe out ammonia and poop it out. Leftover food and dead leaves rot into ammonia too. Ammonia burns gills and can kill a fish in a day.

B

Bacteria eat the ammonia and turn it into nitrite. Good news, but nitrite is still poison.

C

A second kind of bacteria eats the nitrite and turns it into nitrate. Nitrate is far gentler — fish live happily with a little of it.

D

Nitrate leaves in two ways: plants drink it up as fertiliser, and you pour it down the sink when you change water. If you do neither, it slowly climbs and feeds algae.

Why it is called a "cycle": nitrogen keeps changing form and moving — from food, to fish, to ammonia, to nitrite, to nitrate, to plants (or the sink) and back into new food. Nothing is destroyed, it just changes shape.
Part B · the four players

03 · ammonia — where does it come from?

Ammonia is not something you add — it is made inside the tank, every hour, by everything alive and everything rotting.

01

Fish make it just by breathing. People turn protein waste into urea and pee it out. Fish take a shortcut: they push ammonia straight out through their gills into the water. So a fish is leaking ammonia all day long, even if it never poops.

02

Rotting things make the rest. Uneaten food, poop, dead leaves, a dead snail hidden behind a rock — bacteria break them down and ammonia comes out. Overfeeding is the number one cause of ammonia in a beginner's tank.

03

It comes in two forms. NH₄⁺ (ammonium) is fairly harmless. NH₃ (free ammonia) is the killer. Warm water and high pH turn more of it into the killer form — the same 0.5 ppm reading is nearly harmless at pH 6.5 and dangerous at pH 8.2.

04

What it does to a fish: burns the gills, so the fish cannot take in oxygen. It gasps at the surface, its gills look red or ragged, it stops eating, it sits still with clamped fins. Long exposure at low levels stunts growth and invites disease.

05

The safe level is zero. Not "low" — zero. Anything above 0.25 ppm needs action today: a water change plus a dose of Prime (section 18).

04 · the nitrifying bacteria — your invisible staff

They are not a cloud in the water — they are a sticky, slimy film glued to things. Ninety percent of them live in the filter.

01

Who they are. Two teams. Team 1 (mostly Nitrosomonas) eats ammonia and makes nitrite. Team 2 (mostly Nitrospira; older books say Nitrobacter) eats nitrite and makes nitrate. Neither team can do the other's job.

02

Where they come from: they arrive by themselves. These bacteria are everywhere — in the air, in soil, on plants, on gravel, in tap water, on the fish. You never have to buy them. Once there is ammonia in a warm, oxygen-rich tank, a few land, start eating and multiply. The waiting is the only hard part.

03

Where they live: stuck to surfaces. They glue themselves down in a slimy layer called biofilm. Roughly: filter sponge and filter media most of all, then gravel, then decorations, glass and plant leaves. Only a tiny number drift in the water — so throwing away water does not throw away your cycle.

04

They need oxygen and flowing water. Water flowing through the sponge delivers their food and their air. Switch the filter off for a few hours and the ones deep inside start to suffocate — which is why you never leave a filter unplugged overnight.

05

They grow slowly. Most bacteria double every 20 minutes. These double roughly once a day. That single fact is why cycling a tank takes weeks instead of an afternoon, and why the colony cannot suddenly cope with twice as many fish.

Three ways beginners kill them by accident: rinsing the filter sponge under the tap (chlorine kills them — rinse it in a bowl of old tank water instead); throwing away the old sponge and fitting a brand-new one (you just binned the colony — run old and new together for a month); and leaving the filter off for hours, or scrubbing the gravel, glass and filter all on the same day.

05 · nitrite — the sneaky middle step

This is why nitrite poisoning is called "brown blood disease". An air stone will not save a fish from it — the oxygen simply cannot get on board.

01

Nitrite (NO₂⁻) appears a week or two after ammonia, once Team 1 bacteria get going. Seeing nitrite for the first time is good news — it means your cycle has started. It is also the most dangerous moment for fish.

02

It attacks from the inside. Nitrite crosses the gills into the blood and locks onto the part of the blood that normally carries oxygen. The fish drowns in a tank full of air.

03

Safe level is zero, same as ammonia. The nitrite stage usually lasts longer than the ammonia stage because Team 2 bacteria are the slowest growers of all.

04

If it shows up in a tank with fish: change 30–50% of the water, dose Prime for the whole tank, stop feeding for a day or two, and test again tomorrow. Do not add more fish until it reads zero.

06 · nitrate — and how plants help

Nitrate is the only one of the three that you control with a bucket instead of with bacteria.

01

Nitrate (NO₃⁻) is the end of the line. Normal tank bacteria cannot break it down any further, so it just piles up — the classic sign of a closed loop.

02

It is much gentler than the other two, but not harmless. Above about 40 ppm fish get stressed and grow slowly; shrimp and baby fish suffer earlier. High nitrate also feeds algae. Aim for under 20 ppm.

03

Plants treat it as free food. Nitrogen is the "N" in plant fertiliser. Plants actually grab ammonia first (it costs them less energy), then take nitrate. A well-planted tank can hold nitrate near zero.

04

But plants do not replace water changes. They only take the nutrients they need, and they leave everything else behind — phosphate, minerals, leftover medicine, fish hormones. Plants lower the workload; the bucket still finishes the job.

Scoop out the extra floaters each week — that handful of green is exported nitrate.
Part C · starting a new tank

07 · cycling the water — and how long it takes

"Cycling" means growing the bacteria before the fish arrive, by feeding ammonia to an empty tank. Every beginner who skips this loses fish.

01

Set the tank up completely and switch it on. Gravel, filter, heater at 26–28 °C, decorations, plants, lid. Fill it with tap water treated with Prime (chlorine would kill the bacteria you are trying to grow). The filter runs 24 hours a day from now on, for ever.

02

Feed the empty tank. The bacteria need ammonia, and with no fish there is none. Pick one: a few drops of pure ammonia (no soap, no perfume) to reach about 2 ppm; or a pinch of fish food every day and let it rot; or a raw prawn in a mesh bag. The bottle of ammonia is cleanest and easiest to control.

03

Test every day or two and write the numbers down. Week 1: ammonia climbs. Around week 2: nitrite appears and ammonia starts to drop — Team 1 has arrived. Around week 3–4: nitrite peaks (it can go off the top of the chart) and nitrate appears — Team 2 has arrived.

04

The finish line has a clear test. Add ammonia to 2 ppm in the evening. Test 24 hours later. If ammonia = 0 and nitrite = 0 and nitrate is above zero, the tank can now process a full day's waste overnight. That is a cycled tank. One good day is a fluke — repeat it twice to be sure.

05

Then do a big water change and add fish soon. Nitrate will be high after cycling, so change 50% or more. Add the fish within a day or two — if the tank sits with no ammonia the colony starts to starve and shrink within a few days.

06

How long? Usually 3 to 6 weeks from scratch. Two weeks if you borrowed bacteria from a running tank. Eight or more if the water is cold, the pH is low, or you keep changing the water and washing the ammonia away.

Already have fish in an uncycled tank? That is a "fish-in cycle" and it is a race. Test daily. Whenever ammonia or nitrite goes above 0.25 ppm, change 30–50% of the water. Dose Prime for the whole tank volume every 24–48 hours — it makes ammonia and nitrite harmless while still leaving them as food for the bacteria. Feed once every two days, tiny amounts. Add no more fish. It usually takes 4–8 weeks.

08 · how to speed it up

Bacteria cannot be rushed — but they can be borrowed. Every shortcut below is really "bring bacteria that already exist".

01

Borrow from a running tank (the big one). A squeezed-out sponge, a cup of used gravel, a spare filter cartridge or even a rock from a friend's healthy tank carries millions of ready-made bacteria. Keep it wet and move it across the same day. Only take it from a tank with healthy fish — you can bring disease and pest snails too.

02

Bottled bacteria help, some brands more than others. Seachem Stability, Fritz Zyme 7 and Dr Tim's One & Only are the usual picks. Buy a bottle that has not been sitting in a hot shop for a year, and keep dosing it for the full week it says. Treat it as a head start, not magic — keep testing.

03

Keep it warm: 26–28 °C. These bacteria grow roughly twice as fast at 28 °C as at 20 °C. Turn the heater up during cycling, then back to normal before the fish go in.

04

Give them lots of oxygen. Turning ammonia into nitrate uses a surprising amount of oxygen. An air stone, or a filter outlet that ripples the surface, can cut days off the wait.

05

Watch pH and keep it near 7.5–8.0. The cycle eats up the water's buffer (KH) and slowly drags pH down. Below about 6.5 the bacteria go on strike and the cycle stalls — everyone's "stuck cycle". A small water change or a pinch of crushed coral fixes it.

06

Add plants on day one — they carry bacteria in on their leaves and eat ammonia directly. Keep the lights low or off during cycling: bright light slows these bacteria and grows algae in a tank with nothing to eat it.

07

Do not "help" too much. Big water changes during a fishless cycle just wash away the food. Only change water if ammonia or nitrite climbs above about 5 ppm, which is high enough to slow the bacteria down.

09 · when the fish move in

The bacteria colony is always exactly the size of its dinner. Change the dinner suddenly and there is a gap — that gap is a "mini-cycle".

01

Every fish is a small ammonia factory that never switches off. More fish, or bigger fish, or more food = more ammonia. The word for this is bioload.

02

The bacteria grow to match — but slowly. They need 1–2 weeks to double up after the bioload jumps. Add everything at once and you get a mini-cycle: ammonia and nitrite reappear for a week or two in a tank that was perfect yesterday.

03

So add fish in small batches. In a 20 L tank: two or three small fish, then wait two weeks and test before adding the next two or three. It is slow and it is boring, and it is the single best thing you can do for them.

04

Let them in gently. Float the closed bag for 15–20 minutes so the temperature matches. Then add a splash of tank water to the bag every 5 minutes for another 20–30 minutes so the water chemistry matches. Then net the fish out and leave the shop water behind — never pour it in.

05

Expect cloudy water in the first weeks. A white haze a few days after adding fish is usually a harmless bacterial bloom — different bacteria feasting on leftover food. Feed less, do not medicate, and it clears in a few days. Green cloudy water is algae and means too much light.

06

Watch the fish, not just the numbers. Gasping at the surface, sitting on the bottom, clamped fins, red or swollen gills, hiding, or refusing food all mean "test the water now". In a healthy tank fish swim in the open, beg at feeding time and have bright colours.

07

For the first month, test twice a week. Once you have seen ammonia 0, nitrite 0 and a steady nitrate reading for a month, weekly testing is enough.

Part D · who lives in there

10 · hardy fish for a 20 litre tank

20 litres is about 5 US gallons — a nano tank. It is a lovely size for tiny fish and a cruel size for anything else. The rule for choosing: how big does it get, and does it need to swim far? Almost everything sold as "a community fish" outgrows this tank. These do not.

Betta (male)
VERY HARDY · just 1
The classic 20 L fish. Gulps air from the surface, so low oxygen bothers it less. Wants 25–27 °C, gentle flow and a lid — they jump. One male only, never two.
Chili rasbora
SMALL & CALM · 8–10
Only 2 cm grown up, so a proper little school fits. Wants a mature, settled tank with plants — give it a few months of running time first.
Ember tetra
HARDY · 6–8
Glowing orange, peaceful, forgiving about water. Stays around 2 cm. Happiest in a group — a lone one hides all day.
Endler's livebearer
TOUGHEST ON THE LIST · 3 males
Bright, busy and almost unkillable. Buy males only — a mixed pair will fill a 20 L tank with babies in two months.
Pygmy corydoras
GENTLE · 6 minimum
A tiny bottom-dweller that shoals. Needs smooth sand or rounded gravel (sharp gravel wears their barbels away) and a tank that has been running a few months.
Least killifish
VERY HARDY · 4–6
One of the smallest fish in the world (2–3 cm). Happy at room temperature, so often no heater needed. Breeds slowly, a few babies at a time.
Nerite snail
CLEAN-UP CREW · 1–2
Eats algae off the glass all day and cannot breed in fresh water, so it never takes over. Needs a lid — they wander out. Copper kills them (section 13).
Cherry shrimp
TINY WASTE LOAD · 10+
Not a fish, but the best 20 L pet there is. Needs a mature tank and zero copper — the whole story is in section 11.

Pick one of these groups — not all of them. A 20 L tank is full with one betta, or one small school, plus a snail or shrimp.

Goldfish
✕ NEEDS 75 L+ EACH
Reaches 20–30 cm, lives 15 years, and makes more waste than any tropical fish. A bowl or a 20 L tank is a slow death.
Common pleco
✕ GROWS TO 45 cm
Sold as a 5 cm "algae cleaner". It becomes a waste machine the size of your forearm. (A small bristlenose still needs 100 L+.)
Zebra danio
✕ NEEDS A LONG TANK
Hardy, yes — but it sprints all day and needs a tank at least 60 cm long. In a nano tank it paces the glass.
Angelfish, gouramis, barbs
✕ TOO BIG OR TOO BOLD
Angelfish get hand-sized and tall; tiger barbs nip; dwarf gouramis often arrive sick. None of them belong in 20 L.
01

Ask the adult size, not the shop size. Everything in the tank at the shop is a baby. If a fish grows past about 4 cm, it is not a 20 L fish.

02

Length of the tank matters as much as litres. A tall, narrow 20 L is worse than a long, low 20 L, because fish swim side to side, not up and down.

03

"One inch of fish per gallon" is only a rough guess. It ignores the shape of the fish, how messy it is and how much it moves. Use it to say no, never to say yes.

04

Schooling fish need six or more of their own kind. Three tetras is not a school — it is three nervous fish. That is why one species fills a nano tank: six to ten of one kind beats two each of four kinds.

05

Leave room for mistakes. A lightly stocked 20 L forgives a missed water change. A fully stocked one does not.

11 · when there are shrimp

Shrimp are shredders, not filters. They make waste break down faster; they do not make it leave the tank.

01

Cherry shrimp (Neocaridina) are the beginner shrimp. Colourful, cheap, and they breed in a normal tank. Amano shrimp are bigger and the best algae eaters, but their babies need salt water so they never take over.

02

They barely add to the bioload. Ten shrimp make less ammonia than one small fish. A 20 L tank can eventually hold 20 or more. Start with about 10 and let them breed.

03

They graze all day on biofilm — the invisible slime on wood, rocks and leaves. That is why a brand-new, spotless tank starves them. Wait until the tank is about three months old, or add an old leaf or piece of wood from an established tank.

04

They are the canary in your tank. Shrimp die from ammonia, nitrite, copper and sudden swings at levels a hardy fish shrugs off. If your shrimp are unhappy, test the water before you blame bad luck.

05

Shells need minerals. Shrimp grow by climbing out of their old shell and hardening a new one. Water that is too soft (no calcium or magnesium — low GH) causes failed moults, where the shrimp gets stuck and dies. Remineralise very soft or RO water, or drop in a cuttlebone or a mineral block.

06

Change water gently. Shrimp hate sudden change more than they hate slightly stale water. In a shrimp tank do 10–15% weekly instead of 30%, and add the new water slowly — a thin drip or a cup at a time.

07

Most fish eat baby shrimp. A betta will hunt them one by one. If you want a shrimp colony, keep shrimp on their own, or with tiny peaceful fish and a thick mat of moss for the babies to vanish into.

08

Feeding them is easy to overdo. A piece of shrimp pellet the size of a match head, two or three times a week, is plenty — they are already eating your algae. Take out anything left after a couple of hours.

Never put fish medicine in a shrimp tank without reading the label. Most treatments for white spot, velvet and parasites — and every "snail killer" — work by poisoning invertebrates. Shrimp and snails are invertebrates. Treat sick fish in a separate hospital tank instead.
Part E · other things in the water

12 · phosphate — the algae fuel

Algae is not a disease — it is a plant growing where three things are in surplus. Remove any one corner and it fades.

01

Phosphate (PO₄) comes in with the food. Fish food is full of it, and so is fish poop. Some tap water has phosphate added at the waterworks to protect the pipes. It also leaks out of dead leaves and dying plants.

02

It is not poison. Unlike ammonia, phosphate at tank levels does not hurt fish. Plants actually need it to grow — it is one of the three main plant foods (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium).

03

The problem is algae. When phosphate, nitrate and light are all plentiful, algae wins the race — green fuzz on the glass, hair algae on the plants, green cloudy water. Keep phosphate under about 1 ppm in a simple tank.

04

The fix is boring and it works: feed less, scoop out leftovers, change water weekly, keep the light to 6–8 hours on a timer (and away from sunny windows), and grow real plants so they eat the nutrients before the algae can. Phosphate-removing media exists, but it treats the symptom.

05

The API master kit does not test phosphate — it covers pH, ammonia, nitrite and nitrate only. A separate phosphate kit is cheap, but honestly, if nitrate is under control and you feed lightly, phosphate usually follows.

13 · copper — harmless to fish, deadly to shrimp

Fish and shrimp do not share a danger line. A dose that cures a fish wipes out every shrimp and snail in the tank.

01

Why copper is so bad for shrimp. Their blood uses copper to carry oxygen, and their whole body plan is built around it, so extra copper wrecks how they breathe and moult. Fish, which carry oxygen with iron, barely notice the same amount.

02

It does not wash out easily. Copper sticks to gravel, wood and silicone, then leaks back for months. A tank that has been treated with a copper medicine can stay unsafe for shrimp for a very long time — which is why shrimp keepers guard their tanks so carefully.

03

Simple habits that keep it out: use the cold tap only (hot water pulls more metal out of pipes); let the tap run for a minute if the water has been standing; always dechlorinate with something that binds heavy metals — Prime does; read every medicine, algaecide and fertiliser label for "copper", "Cu" or "copper sulphate".

04

If copper gets in: big water changes plus fresh activated carbon, or a resin such as Cuprisorb, will pull most of it out. Move the shrimp to safe water first if you have anywhere to put them.

14 · temperature

The exact number matters less than you think. Steadiness matters more than almost anything else in fishkeeping.

01

Most tropical fish are happy at 24–26 °C. Pick the overlap of everyone living in the tank, then leave it alone. A fish at a steady 24 °C is healthier than one bouncing between 24 and 28.

02

Fast changes cause disease. A sudden drop of 3–4 °C knocks down a fish's immune system, and white spot (ich) usually follows within a week. Slow changes over days are fine; quick ones are not.

03

Warm water holds less oxygen — and at the same time makes the fish breathe harder, the bacteria eat faster and the plants grow quicker. Above about 29 °C, fish hang at the surface. If a heatwave hits: open the lid, add an air stone, turn the lights off, float a sealed bottle of ice water.

04

A 20 L tank needs a small heater — 25 to 50 watts. Do not trust the dial printed on it; hang a separate thermometer at the other end of the tank and set the heater by that. Check it every day for the first week; a heater stuck "on" can cook a nano tank in an afternoon.

05

Where you put the tank is part of the heating. Direct sunlight, a radiator, or an air-conditioner blowing straight at the glass all swing a small tank hard. An inside wall away from windows and doors is the calmest spot.

06

Match the temperature when you change water. New water should be within about 1–2 °C of the tank. In a 20 L tank a bucket of cold water is a third of the whole world changing at once.

Part F · looking after it

15 · how often to feed

Fish always look hungry. They are built to beg — in the wild, food is rare and they eat whenever they can.

01

Once a day is enough for most adult fish. Two tiny feeds a day suit growing young fish and very small fish like chili rasboras — but the total for the day stays the same.

02

Feed what they finish in about two minutes. For six tiny fish in a 20 L tank, that is a pinch you can barely see. Watch them eat. If food reaches the gravel, you fed too much.

03

Take one day off a week. A fasting day lets fish finish digesting and gives the tank a break. Healthy adult fish are also fine for a week with no food at all, so short holidays are no problem — vacation feeder blocks cause more deaths than missing meals do.

04

Mix it up. A good flake or micro-pellet as the everyday food, plus frozen or live treats (daphnia, baby brine shrimp, bloodworm) once or twice a week. Buy small tubs — food loses its vitamins a few months after opening.

05

Scoop out leftovers. Anything not eaten in five minutes is tomorrow's ammonia. A net or a turkey baster takes it straight out.

Overfeeding is the number one beginner mistake — and it never looks like a mistake at the time. It looks like a happy fish eating. The damage arrives three days later as ammonia, cloudy water and algae.

16 · water changes — how often, and the safe way

Ten minutes, once a week. This one habit prevents most of the problems on this page.

Tank
How much, how often
20 L with a few small fish
20–30% (4–6 L) once a week
20 L, fully stocked or messy eaters
25% twice a week
Shrimp tank
10–15% weekly, poured in slowly
Still cycling, with fish inside
30–50% whenever ammonia or nitrite is above 0.25
Neglected for months
15% daily for a week — never one huge change
01

Why bother at all? Nitrate, phosphate, leftover medicine, fish hormones and old organic gunk build up, while the minerals fish need get used up. A water change is the "out pipe" and the "in pipe" of your closed loop in one move.

02

Use a gravel vacuum, not a jug. Most of the mess is hiding in the gravel. Push the wide tube into the gravel, let the dirt lift out, move along. Do a third of the floor each week so you never disturb the whole bacteria bed at once.

03

Always dechlorinate the new water. Chlorine and chloramine kill your bacteria and burn fish gills. Treat the new water in the bucket, or dose the tank for its full volume just before adding the water.

04

Never change 100%. Even in an emergency, 50% is the limit for one go. A total change swings temperature, pH and hardness all at once — that shock kills more fish than the dirty water would have.

05

Clean the filter separately, about monthly. Squeeze the sponge in a bowl of the old tank water you just siphoned out, then put it straight back. Never rinse it under the tap, never replace all the media at once, and never do a filter clean and a big water change on the same day.

06

Keep a fish-only bucket. Soap residue from a kitchen bucket can wipe out a tank. Wash your hands and arms with plain water — no soap, no hand cream — before they go in.

07

Topping up is not a water change. When water evaporates, the salts and minerals stay behind and get more concentrated. Top up with fresh dechlorinated water to replace evaporation, and still do your real water change.

01

Fill to the line properly. Rinse the tube in tank water, fill to the 5 mL mark, and read it at eye level — the bottom of the curved surface should sit on the line. Too much water makes every reading read low.

02

Hold the reagent bottle straight up and down. Tilted bottles give small drops, and the whole test is built on drop size. Squeeze slowly and count out loud.

03

The nitrate test is the one everybody gets wrong. Bottle #2 has a powder that settles into a lump at the bottom. If you do not bang it on the table and shake it hard for 30 seconds before using it, and then shake the capped tube for a full minute after, you will read "low nitrate" in a filthy tank.

04

Read in daylight against the white card. Aquarium lights are blue or pink and will lie to you. Hold the tube flat against the white background on the card, not up against a window.

05

When to test. Every day while cycling. Twice a week for the first month with fish. After that, once a week just before the water change — plus any time a fish looks wrong, after adding fish, and after any medicine.

06

Housekeeping. Rinse tubes with tap water and let them dry upside down (never soap). Keep the caps with the right bottle. Check the expiry date on the box — old reagents read wrong, and the nitrate bottles age fastest.

18 · treating the water with Seachem Prime

Prime is a water conditioner: it makes tap water safe and buys you a day or two in an emergency. It is not a cure and not a filter.

01

Every time you add tap water, add Prime. Dose for the volume of new water (2–3 drops for a 5 L bucket), or dose the whole tank — both are fine, and dosing the whole tank is the safer habit if you pour water straight in.

02

In an ammonia or nitrite emergency, dose for the whole tank. 10 drops for 20 L, repeat every 24–48 hours while you fix the real problem with water changes. Prime does not remove ammonia — it wraps it up so it cannot burn gills, while leaving it available for your bacteria to eat.

03

Do not panic when the test still shows ammonia. The API test measures ammonia whether or not it is wrapped up. Seeing 0.5 ppm after dosing Prime is normal. Judge by whether the number is falling day by day, and by how the fish look.

04

Up to five times the normal dose is safe if you have to, but no more — very large overdoses can lower the oxygen in the water for a while. Keep the surface rippling if you have dosed heavily.

05

It is meant to smell bad. Prime smells of rotten eggs (that is the sulphur that does the work). Store it with the cap tight, out of the sun. A 50 mL bottle will last a 20 L tank for years.

06

It is safe for the bacteria, the plants and the shrimp at normal doses, and it does not change pH.

Part G · cheat sheet

19 · your whole job, in three pictures

Everything else on this page is detail. These three, done steadily, keep a 20 L tank alive.

When
What to do
Every day
Feed once (tiny). Count the fish. Look for gasping, hiding or clamped fins. Check the thermometer.
Every week
Test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH. Change 20–30% of the water and vacuum a third of the gravel. Wipe the glass. Scoop out extra floating plants.
Every month
Squeeze the filter sponge in a bowl of old tank water. Trim plants. Check the heater and filter still work properly. Check your test kit's expiry date.
Never
Rinse the filter under the tap · change all the water · add a new fish the day you see it · use soap · add medicine "just in case" · use copper near shrimp.

Something looks wrong
Check this first
Fish gasping at the surface
Test ammonia and nitrite. Also check the temperature — warm water holds less oxygen. Add surface movement now.
White cloudy water in a new tank
Bacterial bloom. Normal. Feed less, wait, do not medicate.
Green water or green fuzz
Too much light and too much food. Cut the light to 6–8 h, feed less, change more water, add fast plants.
Ammonia appears in an old tank
Something died, you overfed, or you cleaned the filter too hard. Look behind the decorations.
Shrimp dying, fish fine
Copper, a sudden water change, or nitrate too high. Think about anything new you added.
Cycle seems stuck for weeks
pH has dropped below 6.5, the water is too cold, or chlorinated water killed the bacteria.
Fish died soon after being added
Usually the tank was not cycled, or the fish went in without slow acclimation.
The one-sentence version: grow the bacteria before you buy the fish, feed less than you want to, and change a bucket of water every week — because in a closed loop, you are the river.

Numbers here are for a typical freshwater nano tank and are rounded for learning — always follow the instructions printed on your own test kit, conditioner and medicines.
