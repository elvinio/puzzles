Haemoglobin

// the four-seat oxygen ferry in every red blood cell — how it grabs, how it lets go, and why it is red

Every second, about two million of your red blood cells wear out and are replaced. Each one is little more than a soft bag stuffed with a single protein, and that protein's whole job is to pick up oxygen where there is plenty of it and drop it where there is not. The hard part isn't the grabbing — it's the letting go. A glue strong enough to collect oxygen from the lungs would never release it in a muscle. Haemoglobin solves this by being a protein that changes its mind: four seats that talk to each other, a grip that loosens in acid and warmth, and an iron atom that moves about four hundredths of a nanometre and tips the whole molecule from one shape into the other.

Part A · the carrier

01 · water is terrible at carrying oxygen

Oxygen is a non-polar gas and water is a polar liquid, so very little of it dissolves: roughly 0.3 mL per 100 mL of plasma. Haemoglobin doesn't dissolve oxygen — it clips it on, which is why blood carries about 20 mL per 100 mL.

why A molecule of oxygen has no plus end and no minus end for water to grab. It can only slot into the gaps between water molecules, and there aren't many of those. Dissolving is a dead end.
fix So the blood carries a dedicated binding site instead — about 1.2 × 1022 of them in an adult, each one a single iron atom that holds exactly one O₂ and then hands it over. Your oxygen is not dissolved in your blood; it is parked on iron.
sum One gram of haemoglobin carries about 1.34 mL of oxygen. A healthy adult has roughly 15 g in every 100 mL of blood — which is where the 20 mL comes from.

02 · the red cell — a bag with nothing else in it

A mature red cell throws away its nucleus and its mitochondria before entering the bloodstream. That is not a defect — it is the design. Every cubic nanometre goes to cargo, and a cell with no mitochondria cannot burn the oxygen it is carrying.

1 It is mostly cargo. Haemoglobin makes up about a third of the cell by weight — roughly 270 million copies per cell, so dense that the cell's insides are practically a crystal of the stuff.
2 No mitochondria means no conflict of interest. The courier cannot eat the parcel. A red cell gets its own energy by fermenting glucose to lactate, a process that needs no oxygen at all.
3 No nucleus means it is flexible, and doomed. It can fold like a taco to squeeze through a capillary narrower than itself — but it cannot repair itself, so it wears out after about 120 days and some 400 km of travel. You carry about 25 trillion of them.
Scale, to keep in mind. If a red cell were a sports hall, one haemoglobin molecule would be a grapefruit — and there would be 270 million grapefruit in the hall, shoulder to shoulder, with barely room for water between them.

03 · four chains, four seats — the structure

A tetramer: two α chains, two β chains, held together by nothing but shape — no covalent bonds between them. They pair up as two αβ half-molecules that rock against each other like the two halves of a clam.

Each chain folds into the same shape, the globin fold — the same pattern as the myoglobin in your muscles and the leghaemoglobin in a bean root. Unrolled, it is a short string of helices with a pocket in the middle:

Eight helices, about 150 amino acids, and a hydrophobic pocket that fits one flat haem disc. Two residues do almost all the chemistry: the proximal histidine that grips the iron from underneath, and the distal histidine that leans over the empty seat from above.

04 · the haem — one iron atom, held in a claw

Haem is not protein. It is a small, flat, intensely coloured molecule that the cell builds in eight steps and slots into each finished globin chain. Chlorophyll is built on almost the same ring — swap the iron for magnesium and change a few side groups, and red becomes green.

Seen edge-on, the point of the whole arrangement becomes obvious. An iron atom can make six bonds. The ring uses four of them, the protein uses the fifth, and the sixth is left deliberately empty:

Six bonds. Four to the ring, one to the protein, and one seat. The seat is the only part of haemoglobin that oxygen ever touches.

Why the iron has to be wrapped at all. A bare iron atom in water plus oxygen gives you rust — an irreversible reaction. The porphyrin ring and the pocket of oily amino acids around it keep water away, keep two haems from ever meeting each other, and hold the iron in a state where oxygen can land on it and come back off unchanged. Free iron would bind oxygen once. This one does it a few hundred thousand times.
Part B · loading oxygen

05 · the moment of binding

The O₂ touches down on one atom and kinks away at about 120°. That bent shape is not an accident: it is the geometry that a molecule with a double bond's worth of electrons can make with an iron that has a half-filled d-shell — and it is also the geometry that carbon monoxide, which prefers to sit bolt upright, finds uncomfortable.

1 An O₂ molecule wanders into the pocket. There is no door and no energy barrier to speak of — it simply diffuses in through gaps that open and close as the protein breathes.
2 It lands on the iron's sixth site. The iron does not oxidise. It shares electron density with the oxygen while the oxygen is there and takes it back on the way out. Chemists argue about whether to call the bound state Fe²⁺–O₂ or Fe³⁺–O₂⁻; what matters is that it is reversible.
3 The distal histidine makes a hydrogen bond to the far oxygen atom — a light touch that holds the O₂ at the right angle and raises the price of admission for molecules that want to sit straight.
4 The whole visit lasts a few milliseconds to tens of milliseconds. In the time one red cell takes to cross a capillary — about a second — the loading and unloading is comfortably finished.

06 · the click — how a 0.04 nm move flips the whole molecule

With the sixth seat empty, the iron carries four unpaired electrons, which makes it slightly too wide to sit in the hole in the middle of the ring, so it perches above it and the ring domes around it. Binding O₂ pairs those electrons up, the iron shrinks by a few picometres, and it falls into the plane — taking the histidine, and the helix the histidine belongs to, with it.

1 0.04 nanometres. That is how far the iron moves — about a fifth of the width of a single atom. It is the smallest mechanical action in your body that you could call a machine.
2 The iron is bolted to the proximal histidine, which is part of helix F. When the iron drops, the histidine is dragged down with it and the helix has to shift and tilt. A hundredth-of-a-nanometre nudge at the metal becomes a visible shove at the protein's surface.
3 That shove lands on the joint between the two halves of the molecule, where eight salt bridges — pairs of oppositely charged amino acids — are holding everything clamped. The shove breaks them.
4 With the clamps off, the α1β1 half rotates about 15° against the α2β2 half, the central cavity narrows, and every one of the four pockets relaxes into an easier shape. The molecule has two settings, and it has just changed gear.
T  ·  tense, taut, tight-fisted  →  low oxygen affinity
R  ·  relaxed, open, ready to give  →  high oxygen affinity the two shapes differ in affinity for oxygen by a few hundred times

07 · why four seats beat one — cooperativity

Bar lengths are illustrative, not measured — the honest headline is that the last seat is filled a few hundred times more readily than the first. Oxygen is its own signal: every molecule that lands makes the molecule friendlier to the next one.

1 The four seats are not independent. They sit in one rigid frame, so a seat cannot change shape without nudging the other three. Binding at one site is felt at all four.
2 A molecule in the T shape is reluctant: narrow pockets, salt bridges done up, iron domed. One O₂ squeezing in weakens the clamps a little. The second weakens them more. Somewhere around the second or third, the whole molecule flips to R and the remaining seats become easy.
3 The same thing runs in reverse on the way home. Letting one O₂ go makes the next one easier to let go, so unloading cascades rather than trickling.
4 Myoglobin — the single-subunit version in your muscles — has no-one to talk to, so it has no cooperativity. It grabs oxygen harder than haemoglobin and is correspondingly bad at giving it up: it is a store, not a ferry.
The trick in one line. A protein that is bad at binding oxygen when empty and good at it when full behaves like a switch rather than a sponge — and a switch is exactly what you want when the job is "be full here, be empty there".

08 · the S-curve — the most useful graph in physiology

Drawn from the Hill equation with P₅₀ = 26 mmHg and n = 2.8 — the actual numbers for adult human blood at pH 7.4 and 37 °C. The open ring traces one red cell's round trip: all the way up in the lung, all the way down in a working muscle, and back.

flat top Above about 60 mmHg the curve is nearly level. That is a safety margin: you can go up a mountain, or have mediocre lungs, and still fill your haemoglobin almost completely. Breathing pure oxygen barely improves a healthy person's saturation, because there is nowhere left to go.
steep middle Between 40 and 10 mmHg the curve falls off a cliff. Tissue oxygen pressure sits exactly here — so a small drop in local pressure unloads a large amount of oxygen. The busier a tissue is, the lower its pressure, and the more it gets. No sensors, no signalling. Just the shape of a curve.
the S The S shape is cooperativity made visible. A protein with one independent site would give the grey dashed line: a simple hyperbola that is already two-thirds full at pressures where haemoglobin is still nearly empty — and which therefore has almost nothing left to hand over.
Part C · letting go in the right place

09 · the Bohr effect — acid prises the hand open

Christian Bohr, 1904. A muscle that is working hard makes acid, carbon dioxide and heat — and all three are signals that haemoglobin reads as "let go here". The tissue that needs oxygen most is the tissue that causes more of it to be released.

1 Where the acid comes from. Burning fuel makes CO₂; CO₂ plus water makes carbonic acid; very hard work also makes lactic acid. Local pH falls.
2 What the H⁺ does. Protons attach to specific spots on haemoglobin — chiefly the very last amino acid of each β chain and the first of each α chain — and those protonated spots re-form the salt bridges of the T state. Acid physically helps the clamp close.
3 A molecule pushed back towards T has a lower affinity for oxygen, so it drops its cargo. The whole curve slides to the right. Same oxygen pressure, more oxygen delivered.
4 In the lung the logic runs backwards: CO₂ is blown off, pH rises, the curve slides left, and haemoglobin becomes greedy again just in time to load up.
Hb(O₂)4 + n H⁺  ⇌  Hb·Hn + 4 O₂ add acid and the equilibrium runs right — oxygen falls off. Remove acid and it runs left again. Nothing is consumed.

10 · heat, and the wedge in the middle

2,3-bisphosphoglycerate — BPG — is a small, heavily negative molecule that the red cell makes from a by-product of its own glucose metabolism. One molecule per haemoglobin tetramer, sitting in the central cavity, electrostatically glued to positive residues on both β chains. It only fits the T state.

BPG Without BPG, haemoglobin would be useless. Stripped of it in a test tube, it binds oxygen almost as tightly as myoglobin and hands over hardly any. BPG is what tunes a greedy protein down to a generous one.
altitude Go up a mountain and within a day or two the red cells make more BPG. The curve shifts right and the same saturation delivers more oxygen to the tissues. It is one of the first acclimatisation tricks your body plays, long before it starts making extra red cells.
heat Warmth also loosens the grip. Binding oxygen gives off heat, so by Le Chatelier's principle adding heat pushes the reaction backwards. A muscle at 39 °C therefore gets more oxygen than the same muscle at 37 °C — and cold fingers hold onto theirs.
blood bank Stored blood slowly loses its BPG, so fresh out of the fridge it is too good at holding oxygen. It takes a day or so in the recipient to build the BPG back up and start delivering properly.

Part D · getting carbon dioxide home

12 · three ways carbon dioxide rides

Proportions are of the total CO₂ carried. Of the extra CO₂ a tissue actually adds on one pass, the protein-bound share is larger — roughly a third — because that is the part that switches on and off with each oxygen handover.

The most common misunderstanding about haemoglobin. Carbon dioxide is not carried on the iron, and the blood does not swap one gas for the other on the same seat. Oxygen rides on four iron atoms; carbon dioxide rides mostly as bicarbonate in the plasma, and partly on the nitrogen at the far ends of the protein chains. They share a vehicle, not a seat.

13 · the fastest enzyme you own, and a revolving door

Left to itself, CO₂ + water is a sluggish reaction — far too slow for the second a red cell spends in a capillary. Carbonic anhydrase speeds it up by something like a millionfold, turning a bottleneck into a non-issue. It is among the fastest enzymes known.

1 CO₂ slips through the red cell membrane without help — it is small, uncharged and fat-soluble.
2 Carbonic anhydrase converts it to bicarbonate and a proton, almost instantly. Doing this inside the cell is the point: the cell is where the enzyme is, and where haemoglobin is waiting to absorb the proton.
3 The proton is buffered by haemoglobin itself — which is why blood pH barely moves even though you are dumping acid into it continuously. Haemoglobin is your biggest blood buffer as well as your oxygen carrier. And, by §09, taking on that proton is exactly what makes it drop its oxygen.
4 The chloride shift. Bicarbonate can't cross the membrane alone, so the band 3 transporter swaps it for a chloride ion coming the other way — one out, one in, charge unchanged. This keeps the inside of the cell from building up a charge that would stall the whole reaction. Venous red cells are measurably richer in chloride than arterial ones.
5 At the lung, with CO₂ streaming out into the air, every arrow reverses: chloride leaves, bicarbonate comes back in, the enzyme runs the other way, and CO₂ is regenerated and exhaled. No step needs rewiring — only the local concentrations change.

14 · the Haldane effect — each gas helps the other along

J. S. Haldane's half of the partnership. The Bohr effect (§09) says CO₂ changes how haemoglobin holds oxygen. The Haldane effect says the reverse: oxygen changes how haemoglobin holds CO₂. They are two readings of one thermodynamic fact, and each happens at exactly the end of the circuit where it is useful.

why Deoxygenated haemoglobin is a weaker acid than oxygenated haemoglobin — it holds protons more readily. It also forms carbamino bonds with CO₂ more readily. Both properties come from the same T-state shape.
tissue So the very act of handing oxygen over opens up capacity for CO₂ and acid. The cell doesn't have to arrange this; it falls out of the chemistry. Unloading and loading are one event.
lung And oxygen arriving squeezes CO₂ and protons back out, speeding the exhalation. Roughly half of the CO₂ you breathe out is released because oxygen came aboard, not simply because CO₂ is low in the air.
both One mechanism, two famous names. Put simply: a molecule can hold oxygen, or it can hold acid and CO₂, but it does the two jobs in inverse proportion — which is exactly what a two-way ferry needs.
Part E · the impostor

15 · carbon monoxide — same seat, two hundred times the grip

Same iron, same sixth site. CO simply fits it better. The distal histidine penalises CO heavily — a bare haem in solution prefers CO by something like twenty thousand times — so the protein has already knocked the odds down by a factor of a hundred. Two hundred is the number that survives.

1 Why it binds at all. CO and O₂ are almost the same size and both like iron. CO has a lone pair pointing straight out of its carbon atom and an empty orbital to accept electrons back, which makes an unusually strong two-way bond with a metal.
2 What 200× means in practice. Air with just 0.1% CO — one part in a thousand — will eventually take about half your seats, because it is competing against oxygen's 21% with two hundred times the grip. You do not need much CO to be in serious trouble.
3 It is not permanent. Carboxyhaemoglobin slowly comes apart again; the bond is strong, not irreversible. That is the whole basis of treatment (§17).
4 Your body makes a little CO on purpose, breaking down old haem. Everyone walks around with about 0.5–1% of their haemoglobin carboxylated; a heavy smoker may sit at 5–10% all day.

16 · the crueller trick — why losing half your seats to CO is worse than losing half your blood

Both damaged samples carry the same reduced load at the lung. Only one of them can get rid of it. The purple curve is not merely lower — it is bent left, so at a muscle's oxygen pressure it is still clinging to most of what it has.

1 CO takes seats. Obvious, and only half the story. With 50% of the seats occupied by CO, the blood's oxygen capacity is halved — the same as giving away half your haemoglobin.
2 CO also locks the other seats. CO binding is a binding event like any other, so the molecule reads it as "cargo aboard" and flips to the relaxed, high-affinity shape. The remaining oxygen on a half-poisoned tetramer is therefore held far too tightly — the whole curve shifts left and loses its S.
3 Put those together and 50% carboxyhaemoglobin delivers about a quarter of normal oxygen, not a half. This is why CO poisoning kills at saturations a person could survive as anaemia. Someone who has quietly lost half their blood volume can walk; someone at 50% COHb is unconscious.
4 There is a third insult underneath: CO also binds the mitochondrial enzymes that use the oxygen, and the myoglobin in heart muscle. Even oxygen that is delivered becomes harder to spend — which is part of why the heart and the brain suffer first, and why some people are left with lasting harm.
"Carbon monoxide starves you of oxygen." Not quite — and the difference matters. Your lungs work normally, your plasma oxygen is normal, your breathing feels normal. What fails is the delivery: the oxygen is on board and will not get off. You suffocate with your blood nearly full.

17 · why nobody notices — and how it is undone

The half-life figures are typical, not guaranteed — they depend on how much CO is aboard, how hard the person is breathing, and how long the exposure lasted. The shape of the answer is what matters: flood the lungs with oxygen and the seats are won back by weight of numbers.

1 The symptoms are the symptoms of everything. Headache, nausea, tiredness, muddled thinking — the classic presentation is a whole household with "flu" that gets better whenever they go out.
2 A pulse oximeter will lie to you. It judges saturation by comparing red and infrared absorption, and carboxyhaemoglobin happens to absorb red light almost exactly like oxyhaemoglobin. The display reads a comfortable 99% while a quarter of the blood is out of action. Diagnosis needs a blood CO-oximeter, or a CO breath monitor.
3 The treatment is competition. Pure oxygen at ordinary pressure multiplies the O₂ available to fight for each seat; oxygen in a pressure chamber multiplies it again, and also forces enough oxygen into the plasma to keep tissues alive while the haemoglobin is being cleared.
4 The real answer is a CO alarm. Sources: blocked flues, faulty boilers and gas heaters, generators or barbecues used indoors or in a garage, camping stoves in a tent, engines idling near an open window. A cheap detector solves a problem your own senses are simply not equipped to detect.
Part F · why blood is red

18 · colour is what is left over

The ring's twenty-odd carbon and nitrogen atoms share one continuous cloud of loosely held electrons, and a cloud that big absorbs visible light extremely strongly — the 415 nm peak is one of the most intense absorptions in biology. It eats the blue end and leaves the red.

1 Nothing is "making" red. Red is the part of white light that the haem fails to absorb. Take away that absorption and blood would be colourless.
2 It is the ring, not the iron, that carries the colour — but the iron tunes it. Change the metal in the middle of a porphyrin and the colour changes: magnesium gives you the green of chlorophyll, cobalt the pink-red of vitamin B₁₂.
3 It is intense out of all proportion. A single drop of blood in a glass of water is visibly pink. Haem is so good at absorbing light that a few micrograms per millilitre is enough to see.
4 And because the absorption is so strong and so specific, it is also a measurement. Every pulse oximeter, blood-gas machine and lab haemoglobin assay is really just a device for reading the colour (§19).

19 · scarlet and maroon — and how a finger clip reads them

Curves are schematic, drawn to show the shapes rather than exact extinction coefficients. The real oxygenated spectrum has twin peaks at 542 and 577 nm; the deoxygenated one has a single broad band near 555 nm and stays more absorbing all the way into the near infrared.

scarlet Oxygen on the iron changes the electron arrangement around it, which sharpens the absorption into two narrow bands and lets more long-wavelength red escape. Arterial blood therefore looks bright, almost fire-engine red.
maroon With the seat empty the absorption spreads into a single broad band that reaches further into the red, so less red gets back out. Venous blood is dark red — the colour of a good claret.
the clip At 660 nm the two forms differ enormously; at 940 nm the difference is small and reversed. A pulse oximeter flashes both, takes the ratio, and looks it up in a table. It also watches only the part of the signal that pulses, which is how it isolates arterial blood from everything else in your finger.
the flaw The method assumes only two things are present. Carboxyhaemoglobin absorbs at 660 nm almost exactly like oxyhaemoglobin, so it is counted as good news — the single most important limitation of pulse oximetry (§17). Methaemoglobin breaks it differently, dragging the reading towards a stubborn 85%.

20 · veins are not blue

Two effects, stacked. The optics of skin return proportionally more blue than red from the patch directly over a vein; and simultaneous colour contrast — the same trick behind every optical illusion about colour — pushes a slightly desaturated dark red towards blue when it sits in a field of warm pink.

"Deoxygenated blood is blue." It is not, and never is. Cut a vein and what comes out is dark red, like cherry juice. Watch a blood donation: the bag fills with deep maroon, not navy. The blue is a property of skin, not of blood. The diagram in the back of many school biology books — blue on one side of the heart, red on the other — is a labelling convention that has quietly taught generations the wrong fact.
bright scarlet
Arterial blood, ~98% saturated. What you see when you nick a fingertip.
dark maroon
Venous blood, ~75% saturated. What comes out of the needle at a blood test.
chocolate brown
Methaemoglobin. The iron has genuinely oxidised to Fe³⁺ and can hold no oxygen at all.
cherry red
Carboxyhaemoglobin. Brighter than healthy arterial blood — and no use to anyone.
The brown one is worth a paragraph. Normally a tiny fraction of your haemoglobin oxidises to the Fe³⁺ form by accident every day, and an enzyme quietly reduces it back. Certain drugs and chemicals — some local anaesthetics, some antibiotics, nitrites in contaminated well water — overwhelm that enzyme. The blood turns brown, the patient turns grey-blue, and a pulse oximeter sticks near 85% no matter what oxygen you give. The antidote is methylene blue, which hands the iron its electron back. Methaemoglobin is the difference between oxygenation, which is what haemoglobin does all day, and oxidation, which breaks it.
Part G · why blood comes in types

21 · the type is on the wrapper, not the cargo

Blood group has nothing to do with how well your blood carries oxygen. A type O athlete and a type AB athlete have the same dissociation curve, the same P₅₀, the same everything. The types are a matter of what is written on the outside of the envelope.

1 A blood group is a surface marking. Red cells are studded with proteins and sugar chains. Some of those markings vary between people, and where they vary, your immune system can tell self from other.
2 ABO is made of sugars; Rh is made of protein. That difference explains a lot later: sugar antigens tend to provoke one class of antibody, protein antigens another, and the two classes behave very differently in pregnancy (§28).
3 There are more than forty recognised blood-group systems and several hundred known antigens — Kell, Duffy, Kidd, MNS, Lewis, Lutheran and so on. ABO and RhD get the attention because they cause the fastest and most dangerous reactions.
4 The same A and B sugars appear on many other tissues, and about 80% of people also secrete them into saliva and other fluids. Your blood group is not really a property of your blood — it is a property of you.

22 · the ABO sugars — one enzyme, four outcomes

Percentages are rough global averages and vary enormously between populations — type B is much more common across Asia than in western Europe, and some indigenous populations of the Americas are almost entirely type O.

A and B The A and B genes are the same gene, differing by a handful of letters. Those few differences change the shape of the enzyme's business end just enough that it picks up a different sugar to attach. Two versions of one enzyme; two different caps.
O The O version has a single letter deleted. That throws the rest of the gene out of step, and the enzyme it produces is useless. Nothing gets added, so the stem is left bare — which is why O is "recessive": it isn't a third cap, it is the absence of any cap.
AB Inherit an A version from one parent and a B from the other and both enzymes run, so both caps appear. A and B are codominant — neither hides the other.
Rh RhD is a different story entirely: a membrane protein that you either make or do not. "O positive" means no A or B cap, plus the RhD protein present. About 85% of people are RhD positive.

23 · the antibodies you already have

This is the reaction that makes a mismatched transfusion a medical emergency rather than an inconvenience: fever, back pain, dark urine, falling blood pressure, kidney failure. It is also, conveniently, what happens on a glass slide during cross-matching — clumping in the test tube is exactly the result you are looking for.

1 You are born without them. A newborn has essentially no anti-A or anti-B of its own. They appear over the first three to six months of life.
2 Where do they come from, if nothing injected them? The best explanation is that the gut fills with bacteria whose surface sugars look remarkably like the A and B caps. The immune system learns to attack the ones that don't match its own — and incidentally becomes armed against other people's red cells it has never met.
3 So the rule is simply: you carry antibodies against every ABO cap you do not have. A has anti-B. B has anti-A. O has both. AB has neither — which is why AB is the easiest type to receive cells into and the hardest to donate them from.
4 These natural antibodies are mostly IgM — large, five-armed, superb at clumping cells, and too big to cross a placenta. Hold on to that fact; it is the reason mother–baby ABO mismatch is usually harmless (§28).

24 · why blood types exist at all

The honest answer is that nobody fully knows. But the shape of the explanation is well supported, and the clues are strong.

clue 1 The polymorphism is older than our species. Chimpanzees and gibbons have their own A and B types, inherited from a shared ancestor more than twenty million years ago. A variation that has been kept that long is being actively maintained, not merely tolerated.
clue 2 Germs use the caps as docking points. Helicobacter pylori, several norovirus strains, some strains of E. coli, and the cholera bacterium's toxin all interact with these surface sugars. Which sugars you display changes which germs find you convenient.
clue 3 Malaria is the clearest case. Red cells infected with Plasmodium falciparum stick to uninfected ones in "rosettes" that block capillaries — and rosettes form more readily on A and B cells than on O. Type O is associated with a meaningfully lower risk of severe malaria. In a malarial region that is a powerful advantage, and it shows in the map: O is commonest where malaria has been longest.
clue 4 But O is not simply "best". Type O people suffer more severe cholera. The same principle appears elsewhere: people who lack the Duffy protein are resistant to one species of malaria parasite, and in Africa almost everybody lacks it — which is what you expect when a marking is removed because something was using it as a door handle.
so Each type is good against some threats and bad against others, and the threats differ by place and century. No variant can sweep to fixation, so several persist — which is why you have to be typed before a transfusion. Blood groups are not for anything. They are the fossilised record of a very long argument with our parasites.
Part H · a mother and her baby

25 · a baby is not a blend

Blood type is inherited like eye colour, not mixed like paint. Each parent contributes one of their two copies, at random. A type A mother can perfectly well have a type B or type O baby — and a type O mother can have a type A baby if the father carries A.

1 "Type A" does not tell you the genes. It could mean A-and-A, or A-and-O. Someone carrying a hidden O looks exactly like someone carrying two A's, because O adds nothing to see.
2 So the surprises are ordinary genetics. Two type A parents can have a type O child, if each was quietly carrying an O. An O mother and an AB father can never have an O or an AB child — they will have an A or a B.
3 The pattern is reliable enough that blood types were once used in paternity disputes — and unreliable enough on its own that they could only ever exclude a father, never confirm one. (Very rare exceptions exist, like the "Bombay" phenotype, where a person cannot even build the H stem.)
4 And Rh is inherited separately. Two RhD-positive parents, each carrying a silent negative copy, can have an RhD-negative child. This is why a mother and her baby may differ in ABO, in Rh, or in both.

26 · two circulations, one wall

The two bloodstreams come within a few micrometres of each other across millions of tiny fronds, but they stay separate. The baby makes its own blood, of its own type, in its own bone marrow and liver — the mother supplies raw materials, not cells.

1 This is why a different type is no problem. If the two circulations mixed, every ABO-mismatched pregnancy would end in disaster. They don't mix, so almost all of them are uneventful.
2 Gases cross by plain diffusion — no pumps, no carriers. Which means the baby's blood is always working at a lower oxygen pressure than the mother's: you cannot diffuse uphill. Fetal arterial blood runs at around 30 mmHg, a level that would count as alarming in an adult.
3 IgG antibodies are deliberately ferried across by a dedicated transport protein — the mother lends the baby her immune memory for its first months. This is a gift, and occasionally a weapon (§28). The bigger IgM antibodies are not carried and cannot get through.
4 A few fetal cells do leak into the mother's circulation, especially at delivery. Usually harmless. Sometimes the start of a problem that will matter in the next pregnancy.

27 · fetal haemoglobin — a molecule built to steal

Plotted at the same scale as §08. The baby's problem is that it has to get its oxygen second-hand, at pressures an adult would find dangerously low. Its solution is a haemoglobin with a left-shifted curve, so the handover runs downhill all the way.

1 A different chain. Fetal haemoglobin is α₂γ₂ — two α chains as usual, but two gamma chains instead of β. It is a different gene, switched on before birth and off afterwards.
2 And the reason it grips harder is beautifully specific. The γ chain has a serine where the β chain has a histidine, at position 143. That histidine is one of the positive residues that grip 2,3-BPG in the central cavity (§10). Swap it for serine and BPG no longer binds properly — so the wedge that loosens adult haemoglobin's grip barely works on the fetal version. The left shift is not a different chemistry; it is the same chemistry with one of the brakes removed.
3 Two extra helping hands. As the mother's blood gives up CO₂ to the baby's side, her blood becomes more alkaline and grips less, while the baby's becomes more alkaline too and grips more — the "double Bohr" effect, pushing in the same direction. And fetal blood simply contains more haemoglobin per millilitre, around 17 g/dL against the mother's 12–14.
4 Then it is switched off. Production swings from γ to β around birth; by six months HbF is nearly gone, and adults have well under 1%. A newborn's jaundice, and the famous difficulty of interpreting a newborn's oxygen saturation, both trace partly to this changeover.
5 A footnote that became a treatment. Fetal haemoglobin does not sickle. Drugs that coax the γ gene partly back on — hydroxyurea, and now gene therapies aimed squarely at the off-switch — are among the most effective treatments for sickle-cell disease. The molecule a baby grows out of turns out to be the one some people most need back.

28 · when a mismatch does cause trouble

Haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn. Once a leading cause of stillbirth and newborn brain injury, now uncommon wherever anti-D prophylaxis is routine — one of the quiet triumphs of twentieth-century medicine.

1 Why the first baby is usually safe. The mother has to meet the RhD protein before she can make antibodies against it, and the biggest leak of fetal cells happens at delivery. By then the first baby is already out.
2 Why the next one is at risk. Her immune memory persists. In a later pregnancy with another RhD-positive baby, anti-D IgG is ferried across the placenta from early on, coats the baby's red cells and has them destroyed — causing anaemia, jaundice, and in severe cases heart failure before birth.
3 Why ABO mismatch is usually mild, even though it is far more common. Anti-A and anti-B are mostly IgM, which cannot cross the placenta. Group O mothers do make some IgG anti-A and anti-B, which does cross — so ABO disease is real, and it can affect a first baby — but it is nearly always limited to jaundice that phototherapy handles. Fetal cells carry fewer A and B caps than adult ones, and A and B substances are scattered all over other fetal tissues and fluids, soaking up the antibody before it reaches the red cells.
4 The prevention is elegant. Give an RhD-negative mother ready-made anti-D antibody — typically around 28 weeks and again within 72 hours of delivery, plus after any bleeding or procedure. The injected antibody clears the stray fetal cells before her own immune system can register them, so she never builds a memory. You immunise her passively to stop her immunising herself.
5 Other systems can do the same thing — Kell and Kidd antibodies are rarer but can be severe — which is why antenatal care includes an antibody screen, not just a blood type.
So: can a mother and her baby have different blood types? Routinely, yes — it is the normal case rather than the exception, because the baby inherits half its genes from the father. It works because the two circulations never mix: the placenta hands over gases and nutrients, not cells. Trouble arises only when an antibody that can cross the placenta meets a marking on the baby's cells that the mother lacks — and the one that matters most, RhD, is now routinely prevented.
Part I · blood that isn't red

29 · blue blood — copper instead of iron

Haemocyanin evolved entirely separately from haemoglobin and arrived at the same job by a different route. Two copper atoms, held by histidines, clamp a single O₂ molecule between them sideways — not end-on, and not on one metal. Empty, the copper is colourless; loaded, it is a strong blue.

1 It is in the fluid, not in cells. Haemocyanin molecules are enormous — some carry dozens of binding sites on one assembly — and they drift free in the haemolymph. That makes the blood thick and limits how much can be carried, which is part of why it suits slower, cooler animals.
2 It is worse than haemoglobin per litre, and better in the cold. Haemocyanin carries maybe a quarter as much oxygen for the same volume of blood. But cold seawater is oxygen-rich, and haemocyanin keeps working at low temperatures and low oxygen where haemoglobin struggles — which is why it is the standard equipment of the deep sea and the ocean floor.
3 It is cooperative too. Large haemocyanins show the same S-shaped curve and the same sensitivity to pH — evolution reached for the same trick twice, with completely different hardware.
4 Horseshoe crabs are the famous example: a visibly blue animal whose blood is harvested because a clotting factor in it detects bacterial contamination in vaccines with extraordinary sensitivity. That test relies on their immune cells, not their haemocyanin — but the blue is what made people look.

30 · violet blood — iron, but no haem at all

Haemerythrin is the best demonstration that "iron" and "haem" are not the same idea. Same metal as haemoglobin, completely different packaging, and a colour — pink to violet — that is nothing like red. It also shows a pattern: two metal atoms per oxygen, exactly like haemocyanin.

31 · green blood, and no blood pigment at all

chlorocruorin
Green. A haem carrier like haemoglobin, but with one side group of the ring swapped — and that one change moves the colour from red to green. Fan worms and their relatives. Oddly, concentrate it and it looks red again.
green-blooded skinks
A different trick entirely. Some New Guinea lizards have so much biliverdin — a waste product of broken-down haem — that the green swamps the red. Their haemoglobin is perfectly ordinary. It would make a person seriously ill.
Antarctic icefish
No haemoglobin at all — the only vertebrates without it. They lost the genes and get by on oxygen dissolved in plasma, helped by freezing water that holds plenty of it, a huge heart, lots of very thin blood, and no scales.
vanadium in sea squirts
Yellow-green, and a red herring. Some tunicates concentrate vanadium a millionfold. It was long assumed to be an oxygen carrier; the evidence says it probably isn't, and nobody is quite sure what it is for.
One more worth knowing. Some deep-sea tube worms run haemoglobin that carries hydrogen sulfide as well as oxygen, on separate sites, so they can feed the sulfide to bacteria living inside them without poisoning themselves. Their blood is bright red, and does a job no vertebrate's blood does.

Part J · cheat sheet

33 · the whole page, on one screen

The question
The short answer
Why carry oxygen on a protein at all?
Water dissolves almost none of it. Clipping it onto iron multiplies the capacity about 70× — §01
What does haemoglobin look like?
Four protein chains — 2 α, 2 β — each wrapped round one flat haem disc with an iron at its centre. Four seats — §03
How does oxygen attach?
End-on and bent, onto the iron's one free bond. The iron does not rust: it lends charge and takes it back — §05
How can the same molecule grab and release?
The iron slides 0.04 nm into the ring, drags a helix, snaps the salt bridges, and the whole molecule shifts from tight (T) to open (R) — §06
Why four seats instead of one?
They talk to each other. Each oxygen makes the next easier, so the molecule behaves like a switch rather than a sponge — §07
How does it know where to let go?
It doesn't. The curve is steep exactly where tissue oxygen sits, so a small local drop unloads a lot — §08
What makes a busy muscle get more?
It makes acid, CO₂ and heat, all of which loosen the grip — the Bohr effect. Plus 2,3-BPG wedged in the middle — §09, §10
How does CO₂ get home?
~70% as bicarbonate in the plasma, ~23% on the protein's amino ends, ~7% dissolved. Never on the iron — §12
What speeds that up?
Carbonic anhydrase, inside the red cell, at about a million reactions a second — with bicarbonate swapped out for chloride — §13
Do the two gases interfere?
They help each other. Dropping O₂ makes CO₂ easier to pick up, and picking up O₂ pushes CO₂ off — the Haldane effect — §14
What does carbon monoxide do?
Takes the same seat with ~200× the grip, and locks the remaining seats shut. 50% poisoned delivers ~a quarter of normal — §15, §16
Why is CO so dangerous?
No smell, no colour, no breathlessness, skin stays pink, and a finger oximeter reads it as oxygen. Buy an alarm — §17
Why is blood red?
The haem ring absorbs violet, blue and green ferociously. Red is simply what is left over — §18
Why are veins blue?
They are not. Venous blood is dark red. Skin returns proportionally more blue from over a vein, and your eye exaggerates the contrast — §20
What is a blood type?
Sugar caps on the outside of the red cell — nothing to do with the haemoglobin inside. Rh is a protein instead — §21, §22
Why do you have antibodies against other types?
Gut bacteria carry lookalike sugars, so you become armed against every cap you don't have — by about six months old — §23
Why do blood types exist?
Germs use the caps as handles. Each type is good against some and bad against others, so all of them persist — §24
Can a baby be a different type from its mother?
Yes, routinely — it inherits half its genes from the father, and the two circulations never mix — §25, §26
How does a baby take oxygen from its mother?
Fetal haemoglobin (α₂γ₂) grips harder, because the γ chain can't bind 2,3-BPG. Its curve sits to the left of hers — §27
When is a mismatch dangerous?
When an antibody that can cross the placenta (IgG) meets a marking the mother lacks. Mainly RhD — and anti-D injections prevent it — §28
Is any blood actually blue?
Yes — haemocyanin, two coppers instead of an iron, in octopus and crab. Also violet, green, and colourless — §29–§31

The three ideas the whole page rests on
In one line
A seat, on a metal
One iron atom with exactly one bond to spare, wrapped so tightly in protein that oxygen can land on it and leave again. This is how it carries anything at all.
Two shapes, not one
Binding pulls the iron 0.04 nm and flips the molecule between a tight form and an open form. This is why it can be greedy in the lung and generous in a muscle.
Everything is reversible
Oxygen, CO₂, acid, heat, BPG — every one pushes an equilibrium one way at one end of the circuit and the other way at the other. This is why no sensing, signalling or control is needed anywhere.
The one-sentence version: haemoglobin is four copies of a protein that each hold one oxygen molecule on one iron atom — arranged so that filling up makes filling easier, so that acid and warmth and carbon dioxide all make it let go, and so that the places which need oxygen most are exactly the places that take it. The red is an accident of which colours the haem ring happens to swallow, and the blood types are written on the outside of the cell, where the cargo never goes.

Numbers on this page are typical textbook values for a healthy adult at rest, rounded for learning — real measurements vary with altitude, age, fitness, pregnancy and illness, and the curves are drawn from the Hill equation rather than from measured data.
